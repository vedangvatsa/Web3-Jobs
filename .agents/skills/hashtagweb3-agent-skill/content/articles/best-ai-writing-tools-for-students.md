AI writing tools can help students at specific stages of an assignment: turning a vague topic into a clear thesis, organizing an outline, tightening sentences, and formatting citations. They work best when you remain the author and use the tool to support your thinking. This guide covers four tools that are widely used in 2026, what each one actually does, what it costs, where it helps, where it falls short, and how to use it without violating course rules.

## How we chose these tools

We focused on tools with a free entry tier, clear student use, and an official product page that describes features and pricing in August 2026:

- Whether a free version is usable for real coursework, not just a demo
- Whether the tool fits a distinct stage: ideas, structure, language, or citations
- Whether pricing and limits are public and verifiable on the vendor site
- Whether the tool is permitted at many universities for specific tasks when disclosed

No tool writes a finished essay for you in an acceptable way. Every tool below can produce inaccurate or fabricated information if you ask it to generate facts or references. You must verify dates, numbers, and citations against primary sources.

## 1. ChatGPT by OpenAI - Best for brainstorming, explaining concepts, and outlining

### What it is
ChatGPT is a conversational AI assistant from OpenAI. You type prompts and it responds with ideas, explanations, outlines, and feedback on text you paste. It also has a Study Mode designed to walk through concepts step by step instead of giving a direct answer.

### Who it is for
Students who get stuck at the start of a assignment, need to test a thesis, need a plain-language explanation of a difficult concept, or want feedback on an outline they already wrote. It is useful across subjects because you control the prompt. It is less useful if you need verified citations - it will invent references if asked to provide them.

### How it works
You provide a prompt that describes your task, audience, length, and constraints. ChatGPT generates text based on patterns in its training data and, when you enable it, web search. It does not browse your university library by default and does not know your course rubric unless you paste it. The quality of the output depends on how specific your prompt is and on whether you ask it to critique your work rather than produce work to submit.

### What it costs in August 2026
Pricing is public on chat.openai.com/pricing and help.openai.com:

- Free: $0. Includes GPT-5.6 Luna, limited messages with file uploads, limited image generation, voice, deep research, and memory.
- Plus: $20 per month, billed monthly. No annual billing option for this plan according to OpenAI Help Center. Includes GPT-5.6, expanded messages and uploads, more accurate image creation, expanded deep research, expanded memory and context, projects, scheduled tasks, and custom GPTs.
- Pro: Two tiers at $100 per month and $200 per month for 5x and 20x usage compared to Plus, plus access to GPT-5.6 Sol Pro and maximum research and image limits. Most students do not need Pro.
- Go, Business, and Education plans also exist for longer conversations or institutional deployment.

Check the pricing page before you pay, because features and limits change.

### Pros
- Fast at generating multiple thesis options, outline structures, and counterarguments from one prompt
- Good at explaining complex topics at a chosen reading level when you provide the source text
- Study Mode, deep research, file upload, and custom GPTs allow more structured work than a basic chat box
- Free tier is functional for brainstorming and outlining without payment

### Cons
- Fabricates facts, dates, statistics, and citations when asked to provide them. You must verify everything it states.
- Can produce generic phrasing that sounds similar across students if you copy the output directly
- Limited context on Free; longer documents may be truncated or receive slower responses
- Use without disclosure violates academic integrity rules at many institutions

### How to use it well
Write your own rough idea first, then ask for improvement.

Example prompts that keep you as the author:

- Brainstorm: "I need to write a 1,500-word essay on how social media affects teenage mental health. Suggest three distinct thesis statements, each with a different angle, and list one strength and one weakness for each."
- Outline feedback: "Here is my outline: [paste your outline]. Review it for logical flow, gaps, and where a reader would push back. Suggest improvements but do not write paragraphs for me."
- Explanation: "Explain photosynthesis as if I am 15. Use an analogy, then ask me three questions to check my understanding."
- Revision: "Here is a paragraph I wrote: [paste your paragraph]. Flag unclear sentences and suggest two alternative phrasings for each, but keep my meaning."

Keep your chat logs. If your course requires disclosure, you will need the tool name, model, and prompts you used.

## 2. Grammarly - Best for grammar, clarity, and tone

### What it is
Grammarly is a writing assistant that checks grammar, spelling, punctuation, word choice, and tone as you type. It works in a browser extension, desktop app, mobile keyboard, and directly in Google Docs, Microsoft Word, and many learning systems.

### Who it is for
Students who want to catch mechanical errors and make sentences clearer without changing their argument. It is especially helpful for non-native English writers and for students submitting formal academic prose where tone and correctness matter.

### How it works
Grammarly scans text you write and suggests edits with an explanation for each suggestion. It flags issues like misplaced commas, subject-verb agreement, wordy sentences, passive voice, and inconsistent tone. It also includes a plagiarism checker that compares text against web pages and a detector for AI-generated text. You choose to accept or reject each suggestion.

### What it costs in August 2026
Pricing is public on grammarly.com/plans and support.grammarly.com:

- Free: $0. Includes writing without basic mistakes, tone detection, and 100 generative AI prompts per month.
- Pro (listed as Plus in some regions, including India at about Rs 392 per month billed annually): $12 per month billed annually ($144 per year) or $30 when billed monthly. Includes full-sentence rewrites, tone adjustments, fluent English suggestions, plagiarism and AI detection, consistent citations, and 2,000 AI prompts per member per month.
- Enterprise: Contact sales. Adds administrative controls and security features.

Grammarly also offers a 7-day free trial of Pro and, at times, a 50 percent discount for eligible students verified through SheerID. Availability of the student discount varies by region and time, so check the official student page and verify through SheerID before you assume a discount applies. Many universities also provide campus-wide access through Grammarly for Education, so check your library or writing center first.

### Pros
- Catches errors that basic spell check misses, including punctuation and conciseness
- Explains each suggestion, which helps you learn the rule for next time
- Works across more than one million apps and sites, so you do not need to copy and paste
- Plagiarism and AI detection tools help you check originality before submission
- Many universities explicitly allow grammar checkers like this and treat them similar to spell check

### Cons
- Free tier is limited to basic corrections and a small AI prompt allowance
- Advanced rewrites and plagiarism checks require a paid plan
- Tone suggestions can be too formal or too casual for your actual assignment if you accept them without judgment
- It cannot evaluate your argument, evidence, or logic

### How to use it well
1. Draft in your own words without Grammarly first.
2. Run Grammarly after your draft is complete and review each suggestion. Do not accept all at once.
3. Use the tone detector to check whether an academic essay sounds appropriately formal.
4. Run the plagiarism checker on near-final drafts, not on early notes, to avoid burning your monthly word allowance.
5. Keep the original draft so you can show your process if asked.

## 3. QuillBot - Best for paraphrasing, summarizing, and citations

### What it is
QuillBot is a writing suite that includes a paraphraser, grammar checker, summarizer, translator, AI detector, and citation generator. It started as a paraphrasing tool and now bundles several academic tasks in one account.

### Who it is for
Students who need to restate source material in their own words, shorten long articles to key points, or generate citations in many formats. It is useful when you have already read the sources and need help with clear rewording, not when you want to avoid reading them.

### How it works
You paste a sentence or paragraph and choose a mode. QuillBot returns alternative phrasings that keep the meaning while changing structure and word choice. The summarizer extracts key sentences from long documents, and the citation generator formats references in styles like APA, MLA, and Chicago based on information you provide.

### What it costs in August 2026
Pricing is public on quillbot.com/upgrade, the QuillBot blog, and help articles updated June 2026:

- Free: $0. Paraphraser limited to about 125 words per request, with Standard and Fluency modes only. Summarizer limited to about 1,200 words. AI Detector limited to about 1,200 words and 10 scans per day. No plagiarism checker. Translator limited to about 5,000 characters.
- Premium: $19.95 billed monthly, $39.95 billed every 3 months (about $13.31 per month), or $99.95 billed annually (about $8.33 per month). Includes unlimited paraphrasing, 9 standard modes, compare-modes view, paraphraser history, advanced grammar suggestions, Summarizer up to 6,000 words with custom summary options, unlimited AI Detector, AI chat with higher limits, and plagiarism checker with about 25,000 words per month. Additional plagiarism words can be purchased in packs if you exceed the allowance.
- Premium also includes unlimited AI humanizer and higher translator limits.
- Student pricing at times shows an annual rate from about $6.25 per month for verified students, but you must log in and verify eligibility to see current offers.

Free tier is usable for short tasks. Premium is aimed at students who revise long passages repeatedly or need plagiarism checks in the same product.

### Pros
- Paraphraser offers multiple modes and a compare view to choose phrasing that fits academic tone
- Summarizer handles longer documents on Premium, which helps when reviewing several research articles
- Citation generator supports more than 1,000 citation styles and is free for basic use
- Single subscription covers several tasks that would otherwise require separate tools

### Cons
- 125-word limit on Free paraphrasing interrupts longer revisions
- Plagiarism checker on Premium has a monthly word cap and does not roll over
- Paraphrasing quality depends on your original input. Poor source understanding leads to inaccurate rewording
- Marketing for the AI humanizer can suggest evading detectors. Using any tool to disguise AI-generated text as human work violates academic integrity policies at most institutions

### How to use it well
Use QuillBot to help express your understanding, not to replace understanding:

1. Read the source and note the main idea in your own words first.
2. Use Standard or Fluency for basic rewording, and Formal or Academic for scholarly tone where available.
3. Compare modes side by side and choose the version that keeps the original meaning most accurately.
4. Never paste someone else's text, paraphrase it, and present it as your own without citation. That is still plagiarism.
5. Never use a paraphraser or humanizer to rewrite AI-generated text to hide AI use. Disclose AI use instead.

## 4. Paperpal by Editage (Cactus Communications) - Best for focused academic writing

### What it is
Paperpal is an AI writing assistant built for academic and scientific writing. It is trained on scholarly text and designed to keep references, equations, and technical terms intact while improving language, structure, and consistency. It is owned by Cactus Communications and offered under the Editage family of products.

### Who it is for
Upper-level undergraduates, graduate students, and researchers writing theses, journal manuscripts, or other formal academic documents where structure, citation accuracy, and submission checks matter. It is more specialized than a general grammar checker, so it is less needed for short, informal assignments.

### How it works
Paperpal provides suggestions inside Microsoft Word, Google Docs, and Overleaf through an add-in and Chrome or Edge extensions, plus a web editor. You can also chat with PDFs, ask research questions that return answers with cited sources from its research repository, and run pre-submission checks that flag language, structure, and technical issues before you submit. Unlike tools that generate text from a blank prompt, Paperpal emphasizes language refinement and checks on text you have written.

### What it costs in August 2026
Pricing is public on paperpal.com/pricing and in the Paperpal Help Center article updated July 19, 2026:

- Free: $0. No credit card required. Includes limited language suggestions and basic access to research and editing features.
- Prime: $144 per year ($12 per month billed annually) or $25 per month billed monthly. Designed for regular research and writing needs.
- Pro: $348 per year ($29 per month billed annually) or $59 per month billed monthly. Designed for high-stakes manuscripts, theses, and journal submissions with higher limits across features.
- Student and multi-year discounts and institutional licenses are available at times. Universities can purchase institution-wide access at custom pricing.

Feature limits differ by tier. For example, plagiarism checks, AI detection, translation, and submission readiness checks have monthly word or usage caps that increase with Prime and Pro. Check the pricing page comparison table for the current caps before you subscribe, because these numbers change.

Paperpal states on its site that it has been used by more than 5 million academics and has perfected more than 10 billion words of academic text, with a rating of Excellent on Trustpilot. These are company-reported figures.

### Pros
- Academic-focused language suggestions that avoid changing references, numbers, and technical terms
- Research and cite features that return verified sources and format citations in more than 10,000 styles, which reduces manual formatting work
- Pre-submission checks that scan for about 30 language and technical issues, including structure and reference problems
- Works where you already write, so you do not need to copy text between tools
- Privacy and data handling described for academic use, with institutional options

### Cons
- More expensive than general writing tools if you only need basic grammar help
- Full benefit requires working in Word, Google Docs, or Overleaf with the extension installed
- No tool can evaluate whether your argument is original or important for your field. Reviewer judgment still applies
- Free tier limits mean heavy users will need Prime or Pro

### How to use it well
1. Install the Word add-in or Chrome extension for the editor you actually use.
2. Write a complete draft first, then run language suggestions. Review each suggestion instead of accepting all.
3. Use Research to find and add citations as you write, then verify each citation against the original source.
4. Run plagiarism, AI detection, and submission readiness checks before final submission, and keep the report for your records.
5. For a thesis or journal submission, consider Prime or Pro during the submission period and cancel after if you do not need it year-round.

## How to use AI writing tools without risking academic integrity

In 2026 most universities do not have a single ban. They use tiered permission frameworks that vary by course, assignment, and even assignment stage. Where use is allowed, they require disclosure. Georgia Tech's AI in Academic and Research Contexts Policy from June 2026 and the CASRAI trends guide from July 2026 describe the same pattern: students must name the tool and its version, state the task it supported, and in some courses include prompts and output as an appendix. A human remains fully responsible for the work and must not present AI output as their own.

A detector score alone is not treated as proof. Turnitin's own AI Writing Report guidance says its detector may misidentify human, AI-generated, and AI-paraphrased text and should not be the sole basis for adverse action against a student. Rice, Vanderbilt, Washington State, University College Cork, University of North Florida, Minnesota, Buffalo, and Michigan publish similar guidance: a detector flag can trigger a review, but a fair review also looks at the syllabus rule, assignment instructions, drafts, version history, notes, sources, and a conversation with the student.

Practical steps that satisfy most current policies:

1. Read the syllabus and assignment instructions first. If the policy is unclear, ask the instructor in writing before you use a tool. Some assignments allow grammar help but not idea generation.
2. Do disclosure properly when AI is allowed. State tool name and version, what you used it for, and which sections were affected. Example disclosure note: "I used ChatGPT with GPT-5.6 for brainstorming thesis options and Grammarly for grammar checking. All arguments, analysis, and final wording are my own. Chat logs are available on request."
3. Own every word. You must understand and be able to defend every claim, citation, and argument in the submission. If you cannot explain it without the tool, rewrite it.
4. Write the first draft yourself. Use AI for feedback after your draft exists, not to produce a first draft to submit.
5. Verify every fact, date, statistic, and citation. Paste a suspicious AI-generated citation into your library search or the journal site to confirm it exists before you include it.
6. Keep evidence of your process: notes, outlines, draft history, chat exports, and plagiarism or AI detection reports. If a question arises, this packet shows how the work was produced.

Never copy and paste AI text as your own submission, never paraphrase AI text to hide AI use, and never fabricate a disclosure. Each of these is treated as plagiarism or misrepresentation at most institutions.

## Quick comparison

- For starting and structuring: ChatGPT Free does the most with no cost. Ask it to brainstorm, challenge your thesis, and critique your outline.
- For polishing language: Grammarly Free catches most mechanical errors. Grammarly Pro adds rewrites and plagiarism checks and is worth checking for campus-provided access before paying.
- For restating sources and managing citations: QuillBot Free handles short paraphrasing and basic citations. QuillBot Premium adds longer limits and a plagiarism checker in one place.
- For formal academic manuscripts: Paperpal Prime or Pro adds scholarly tone, citation handling at scale, and submission checks that general tools lack. Use it when you are writing theses or journal papers, not for every short assignment.

Most students combine two tools: a conversational assistant for ideas and structure plus one language tool for clarity and citations. Start with free tiers, test them on one assignment, then decide whether a paid tier is worth it for your workload.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my teacher detect if I used AI?**
Detectors like Turnitin can flag text as likely AI-generated, but their own guidance says a flag alone is not proof and requires human review. Instructors also look for changes in style, unsourced claims, and gaps between your draft history and final submission. The larger risk is not the detector but the course policy: undisclosed or prohibited use is treated as an integrity violation even if no detector is used.

**Which tool should I start with if I am new to AI writing tools?**
Start with ChatGPT Free for brainstorming and outlining, plus Grammarly Free for grammar and clarity. This combination covers the two most common student needs without cost. Add QuillBot if paraphrasing and citations are a regular part of your work, and consider Paperpal only when you need formal academic checks.

**Are these tools free?**
All four have functional free tiers. ChatGPT Free is fully usable for ideas and explanations. Grammarly Free covers basic corrections with 100 AI prompts per month. QuillBot Free covers short paraphrasing and citation generation. Paperpal Free covers limited language suggestions. Each also sells a paid plan with higher limits and additional checks. Check your university portal first, because many schools provide Grammarly or other tools at no direct cost to students.

**Will using AI hurt my writing skills?**
It depends on how you use it. If you use AI to catch errors, test your outline, and learn from explanations for each correction, it can strengthen revision skills. If you use it to generate drafts you do not revise or understand, it weakens the practice you need to think and write clearly. A useful test is whether you can explain every paragraph of your final paper without looking at AI output.

**How should I cite or disclose AI use?**
Follow your course instructions exactly. Where disclosure is required, name the tool and version, describe the task (for example, brainstorming or grammar checking), and note which sections were affected. Some courses ask for a short disclosure statement at the end of the paper and a copy of prompts and output as an appendix. Journals and many universities treat AI as a tool that cannot be an author, so you cite its contribution in your methods or acknowledgments rather than listing it as a co-author.

**What should I do if my course bans AI use?**
Follow the ban for that course, even if other courses allow limited use. Do not use grammar checkers with generative features either if the ban includes them. Use university writing centers, peer review, and office hours for feedback instead.

**Are AI-generated citations trustworthy?**
No. AI tools frequently produce citations with wrong years, journal names, or page numbers, or citations to papers that do not exist. Use AI to format a citation only after you have confirmed the source exists and you have read it. Always check a citation against the publisher site or library database before you include it.
