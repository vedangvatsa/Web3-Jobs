## Introduction: Bitcoin first

Jack Dorsey's argument about [Web3](/what-is-web3) begins from a narrow premise: a decentralized internet needs a neutral monetary network before it needs more tokens, marketplaces, or social applications. In that frame, Bitcoin is the relevant network. The rest of the crypto market is not a peer set to be surveyed and ranked. It is a set of projects that must clear a high bar on ownership, governance, and the ability of an ordinary participant to verify the rules.

That position is often shortened to "Bitcoin maximalism," but the shorthand misses its practical side. Dorsey has used it to criticize venture-financed token networks, to argue for small and stable protocol surfaces, and to direct work at Block and its former TBD unit toward identity and personal data tools. His proposal is not that every web application should be placed on Bitcoin. It is that a web stack should avoid creating a new issuer, a new privileged allocation, or a new company-operated identity silo when open standards can do the job.

The distinction matters because "Web3" is not a technical specification. It has been used to describe public blockchains, token sales, wallet-based login, NFTs, DeFi, creator platforms, and a general promise of less dependence on large internet companies. Dorsey's criticism targets the gap between that promise and a system in which early investors, foundations, administrators, or hosted infrastructure providers retain decisive power. His test is institutional as much as technical: who received the asset first, who can change the rules, and what does a user have to trust to participate?

## The argument about ownership

Dorsey became a prominent critic of the Web3 label during the 2021 wave of token-funded projects. His central objection was not that software builders should be paid. It was that a project cannot credibly present itself as user-owned while large, identifiable groups have preferential claims on the asset, the company, or the essential infrastructure. That claim applies even if the project uses a public blockchain and exposes some code under an open-source licence.

There are several separate questions inside that objection. A protocol can be open source while its most-used interface is run by one company. A token can trade publicly while a concentrated group of investors has a large enough position to shape governance or market incentives. A decentralized application can place state on a chain while relying on a hosted indexer, a domain name, a wallet provider, or a legal entity that users cannot easily replace. None of those arrangements automatically makes a system fraudulent or useless. Dorsey's point is that they should not be described as the same kind of decentralization as a network that has no issuer or privileged token allocation.

That is a more demanding standard than "users can connect a wallet." A wallet address can authenticate a signature. It does not establish that the user owns an application, controls its ranking rules, can take their social graph elsewhere, or has meaningful influence over a protocol upgrade. Dorsey's criticism therefore reaches beyond cryptocurrencies. It is also a complaint about product architecture and the familiar internet habit of calling an account portable while the service keeps the data, the audience, and the rules.

The legal context also complicates claims that a token sale is permissionless capital formation. The SEC's now-withdrawn 2019 framework described the factors it examined when a digital asset was sold with an expectation of profit from the managerial efforts of others, including whether a promoter played a central role in development, governance, liquidity, or marketing. The agency says the framework was superseded in 2026, but its description of those arrangements remains a useful record of why a token's label alone cannot settle the question of control or legal treatment in the United States. See the [SEC's notice and the archived framework](https://www.sec.gov/corpfin/framework-investment-contract-analysis-digital-assets).

## Why Bitcoin occupies a special place

Bitcoin's original paper described a peer-to-peer electronic cash system using proof of work and a chain of signed transactions. The [paper as preserved by Bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/bitcoin-paper) does not promise identity, storage, social media, or programmable markets. That limited scope is part of the attraction for Dorsey. A system designed to do fewer things exposes fewer policy choices and fewer places where a group of operators can add discretion.

Bitcoin also differs from many later token launches in its origin story. It did not begin with a venture round, an initial allocation to a corporate treasury, or a sale of tokens before a network existed. That does not prove that every present-day holder has equal influence; mining equipment, exchanges, wallet providers, and custodians all create real concentrations of power. It does mean that arguments about its early distribution and its creator are structurally different from arguments about a network financed through an insider allocation.

Dorsey's preference for Bitcoin is also a preference for cautious change. The protocol has changed over time through a public, contentious process, but its culture treats compatibility and verification as expensive assets. That can make development slow. It can also make a rule harder to alter for the benefit of a product team or a commercial sponsor. For Dorsey, this is not an accidental inconvenience to be engineered away. It is part of the security model for money.

The trade-off is concrete. Bitcoin's base layer does not offer Ethereum's general-purpose execution environment. Ethereum's original white paper proposed a blockchain with a programming language for contracts and applications, and it openly described the design goal as broader than a single currency. The [Ethereum white paper](https://ethereum.org/en/whitepaper/) also describes the costs of that generality: every execution rule, state transition, and fee mechanism becomes part of a much larger system. Dorsey's position is that applications needing those capabilities should not be allowed to turn that technical flexibility into an unexamined claim of political decentralization.

## Decentralization is not a product badge

It is tempting to treat decentralization as a yes-or-no property. In practice it is a collection of design choices. Can a user validate transactions independently? Can they choose a client? Can they move their data? Who publishes the software updates? Is there a foundation, company, multisignature group, or small group of service providers with emergency control? Are transactions inexpensive enough that participation is more than theoretical? The answer may differ at the protocol, application, and interface layers.

Dorsey's criticism is strongest when it insists on naming those layers. "On-chain" is not a substitute for describing who runs the gateway. "Governed by token holders" does not say how voting power is distributed. "Self-custody" does not tell a reader whether the user can recover a lost key, avoid phishing, or use the service without a centralized identity provider. A careful description of a Web3 product should state what is decentralized, what remains operated, and what the user must trust.

That standard does not require rejecting every company. Block itself is a public company with shareholders, executives, and conventional products. Dorsey's argument is narrower: the company should not be the owner of a monetary protocol or the gatekeeper of a person's identity and data. A company may build a wallet, developer library, or interface around open systems, but the claims need to match the degree of dependence that remains.

It also leaves room for a plain disagreement. Many builders accept investor-led development because capital can fund research, security work, support, and distribution before a network has enough users to sustain itself. They may consider a foundation or elected governance group a workable compromise. Dorsey does not deny the trade-off. He objects to presenting that compromise as the elimination of intermediaries.

## From Square to Block

Dorsey's business decisions put that philosophy into a corporate setting. He resigned as Twitter's chief executive in November 2021; Twitter's board said at the time that Parag Agrawal would become CEO and that Dorsey would remain on the board through the 2022 annual meeting. The company's [announcement](https://blog.x.com/en_us/topics/company/2021/jack-dorsey-resigns-as-twitter-ceo) records the leadership change. Soon afterward, Square adopted the name Block, a move that connected its payment business with Cash App, TIDAL, TBD, and Bitcoin-oriented work.

The renaming did not make Block a decentralized protocol. It made its strategic vocabulary more explicit. The company sells financial products and operates services subject to law, policy, and company decisions. Its Bitcoin work has included wallet and payment efforts, developer infrastructure, and an emphasis on open protocols. The useful question is not whether Block is itself decentralized. It is whether the products it funds reduce dependence on a Block-controlled database or account.

That is why Dorsey's Web3 commentary cannot be separated from his older interest in public conversation. Twitter made communication globally accessible but housed identities, follows, moderation decisions, and distribution inside one service. Bitcoin offered a different example: a common network whose transaction rules are public and whose participants can run their own software. Web5 was an attempt to bring some of that model to identity and data without pretending that a payment ledger should become a social network database.

## Web5 and decentralized web nodes

TBD introduced Web5 as a set of web technologies rather than another blockchain or another token. The current JavaScript implementation describes the stack as libraries for decentralized identifiers, verifiable credentials, and decentralized web nodes. Its [public repository](https://github.com/decentralized-identity/web5-js) is a useful primary record of the project's components and its use of ordinary web runtimes such as browsers, Node.js, and React Native.

A decentralized identifier, or DID, is an identifier designed to be created and controlled without a single central registrar. A verifiable credential is a signed statement that software can check against an issuer's public key. A decentralized web node, or DWN, is a proposed data store and message layer that allows an application to request access under rules set by the identity holder. Those terms describe mechanisms, not results. A DID does not prove a real-world identity by itself, a credential remains only as reliable as its issuer, and a DWN still has to be hosted somewhere.

The intended flow is different from a typical "sign in with" arrangement. Instead of an application creating the master copy of a profile and asking a person to return to that app, a person could hold a DID and grant an application permission to read or write a defined category of data. A second application that understands the same standards could request access to the same data. In principle, that reduces switching costs because the data relationship is not confined to the first product's database.

Bitcoin's role in this model is deliberately limited. The Web5 software repository documents DIDs and DWNs; it does not describe putting personal records on the Bitcoin blockchain. That restraint is essential. A public, replicated ledger is a poor place for private messages, health records, contact lists, or a mutable social profile. Using Bitcoin as an anchoring or payment layer, where appropriate, is a different technical claim from using it as the storage layer for an individual's life.

The work also illustrates a tension in Dorsey's vision. A standard can be open while early development is funded and coordinated by a company. The project can only meet its portability promise if independent clients, hosts, and applications adopt interoperable implementations. Source code on GitHub is evidence that software is available for inspection and reuse; it is not evidence that a broad market has adopted the protocol or that every implementation will be compatible.

## The test Dorsey sets

Dorsey's contribution to the Web3 debate is less a product roadmap than a discipline for reading claims. Ask whether a token was distributed under equal rules. Ask who can change the client, the validator set, the interface, and the policy. Ask whether data can be retrieved in a usable format and whether an alternative provider can serve it. Ask whether a person holds the credential and key material, or merely has an account with a company that can revoke access.

That discipline also corrects an overstatement often attached to self-custody. Holding a private key gives its holder authority to authorize transactions under the relevant protocol. It does not insure against device loss, fraud, irreversible mistakes, price changes, legal obligations, or failures in the software a person chose. It shifts a form of control and a set of risks from a custodian to the holder. That is a meaningful distinction, and it is more useful than treating "not your keys, not your coins" as a complete account of user safety.

The same caution applies to open-source code. Public code can be reviewed, forked, and audited, but a user still has to know which version is running, where their data is stored, and whether the service's economic model creates a new choke point. Dorsey's strongest argument is not that Bitcoin solves every one of those questions. It is that a decentralized-web project should answer them plainly rather than use the word decentralization as a substitute for an answer.
