The United Arab Emirates has moved the Digital Vault inside its national identity platform onto a dedicated Avalanche Layer 1, according to a Sept. 14 announcement from Avalanche. The Vault serves UAEPASS, the country's national digital identity system used by about 12.5 million people to verify identity, sign documents, and reach government and private-sector services. The transition was [reported as confirmed by federal regulator TDRA and Ava Labs](https://crypto-economy.com/uae-moves-national-digital-identity-vault-onto-avalanche-blockchain/), and Avalanche's official account [posted the news on Sept. 14](https://coinlaw.io/avalanche-uaepass-vault-12-5-million-users/).

UAEPASS operates under TDRA, the Telecommunications and Digital Government Regulatory Authority. Citizens, residents, and visitors use it to authenticate, sign documents, and access services online. Inside it sits the Digital Vault, the component that issues, stores, and shares verified documents such as trade licenses, degrees, and address proofs. The platform's own figures put usage at 12.5 million people with more than 15,000 services and over 350 connected government and private-sector entities.

The technical design gives the Vault blockspace of its own. A dedicated Avalanche L1 means institutional transactions do not compete for compute with unrelated decentralized applications or suffer congestion from public network traffic. TDRA keeps control over network membership, permissions, and configuration. According to Crypto Economy's reporting, the stated objective is to turn document verification into an automated direct query, replacing the manual audits each institution currently performs in isolation. Saeed Belhoul, TDRA's director of digital government operations, said the platform needed a scalable environment for growing credential demand, and that the authority retains full governance over consensus rules, authorized validators, and privacy parameters. John Nahas, chief business officer at Ava Labs, said national identity systems demand deterministic execution and low latency, with dedicated blockspace keeping response speeds steady during spikes in authentication requests. A document used across 350 institutions needs trust in its issuer without a fresh investigation by each one, which turns verification into a routine lookup rather than a repeated check. Cryptography settled its half of that problem years ago. The harder half is an issuer every institution already accepts, which is why national identity platforms keep becoming the places reusable credentials get used. Shared blockspace struggles at that scale for a plain reason: a verification at a bank counter cannot wait behind unrelated traffic, and throughput cannot be set by someone else's activity. Control weighs heavily because the accountability is statutory, with 12.5 million people depending on the records, and government procurement moves slowly with heavy documentation for exactly that reason.

One qualification surrounds the announcement. TDRA's own UAEPASS pages describe the Vault as powered by blockchain without naming a protocol, [CoinLaw noted in its Sept. 14 coverage](https://coinlaw.io/avalanche-uaepass-vault-12-5-million-users/). That leaves several questions open for anyone relying on a UAEPASS document as identity assurance: which chain identifier the Vault writes to and who operates its validators, whether document hashes, full credentials, or only revocation state go onchain, what audit covers the contracts and who performed it, and when the migration completed. Startup Fortune [raised the same gap on Sept. 14](https://startupfortune.com/uaes-digital-vault-may-run-on-avalanche-but-no-one-will-confirm-it/), reporting that neither the government nor Avalanche Labs had confirmed the chain behind the Vault in its own channels. The announcement establishes Avalanche's account of the infrastructure. It does not by itself establish an independently verifiable operating record. Startup Fortune added two details that cut in opposite directions. On one side, the government's UAE Verify pages explain the underlying model in plain terms: document contents and personal data are not stored onchain, only a hash used to prove a document is legitimate and unaltered. On the other, Deca4, a Dubai advisory and implementation firm, lists a UAE PASS technical study covering 11 million users while separately naming Avalanche as a technology partner, a specific clue that still stops short of confirming documents are verified on an Avalanche network. The same report pointed to a July precedent in Japan, where Progmat moved more than 452 billion yen in tokenized securities from Corda 5 to a dedicated Avalanche Layer 1, a migration covered with named counterparties and figures.

The deployment follows a precedent in North America. California's Department of Motor Vehicles moved 42 million vehicle titles onto a DMV-run Avalanche chain in July 2024, with title transfers falling from roughly two weeks to minutes after digitization, according to both reports. Avalanche presents the two projects as parallel answers for records at national scale, one for identity and one for property. Crypto Economy reported that the UAE procurement ran through a federal public tender under the country's data sovereignty standards, and that the deployment is already in an active operational phase rather than a pilot, with additional ministries and commercial agencies onboarding through the fourth quarter of fiscal year 2026.

For the institutions downstream, the confirmation gap matters now. Entities accepting UAEPASS credentials as identity assurance rely on an infrastructure layer whose operator has not described it publicly. Belhoul said the upgrade moves the Vault onto infrastructure built to carry growing demand for years to come. Holders face no action. CoinLaw reported that UAEPASS users will see no change in how they request, hold, or share verified documents, and that for the 350 connected entities the useful record remains the Vault's own sharing log of which institution received which document and when.
