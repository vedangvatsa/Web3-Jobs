Binance said it obtained a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing injunction from the Abu Dhabi Global Market courts as it tries to recover crypto stolen in the Oct. 29 hack, the largest recorded in the industry. The court orders target unidentified perpetrators of the attack and are meant to help issuers, exchanges and other partners freeze and return stolen assets, the company said in a statement shared with [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/binance-says-won-65b-worldwide-freezing-order-after-hack) on Nov. 18.

The orders can be served through blockchain transactions, M. Kendall Day of Gibson Dunn, which represents Binance, told Cointelegraph. Legal notices can go directly to wallet addresses instead of traditional channels, and market participants would receive updates to a "living list" of 26,000 wallet addresses identified as holding stolen assets, Day said. The $6.5 billion figure is not a tally of recovered assets, and the statement did not disclose how much has been returned so far under the orders.

Binance deputy general counsel Dugan Bliss confirmed the order in a statement, but gave no terms, [Bitcoin Magazine reported](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/news/binance-abu-dhabi-order-recover-frozen) in its account of the Reuters exclusive. Neither the exchange nor the wire said which assets the order applies to. "We will continue using every lawful tool available to trace, freeze and recover stolen assets, support our partners in returning funds where possible, and keep those assets out of the hands of the attackers," Bliss said. The exchange has not said which assets the order covers.

The scale of what has already been stopped is smaller. Binance has said about $2.85 billion of the stolen tokens were frozen or intercepted after the attack, roughly 45% of the $6.34 billion it says was affected, according to its own incident report cited by Bitcoin Magazine. Most of that sits with stablecoin issuers: Tether said it froze more than $1.5 billion in USDT within 30 minutes of the attack, and Circle said it froze about $1.1 billion in USDC with law enforcement, both accounts said. Circle added that it does not decide who owns frozen assets, a question it left to law enforcement and the courts.

The U.S. Justice Department separately obtained court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the stolen crypto, part of more than $2.7 billion that the department says government and private-sector efforts have frozen. Criminal Division head A. Tysen Duva said the department helped "immediately halt about half of this historic breach," according to the Bitcoin Magazine account. The FBI has attributed the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, which Pyongyang denies.

The attack itself unfolded over months. Binance says a tampered software installer compromised one engineer in June, and in September another engineer was approached by a fake recruiter and talked into running a bogus interview assignment that installed malware. From there, the attackers reached an internal wallet-signing service and drained Binance hot wallets across multiple blockchains on Oct. 29. The thefts included 233.46 million XRP, about 970,500 SOL and 18,270 BTC, the company said. Blockchain analysts found the attackers swapped stablecoins into ether over about 20 minutes to outrun the freezes, but the issuers moved faster, Bitcoin Magazine reported. Tether and Circle can block their own tokens, but bitcoin has no issuer, so the stolen BTC is covered by those freezes only if it reaches a service that blocks it.

Separate interim orders under the ADGM action cover assets that certain named parties froze against whom no wrongdoing is alleged, Cointelegraph reported. Some businesses require formal court orders before they can release, transfer or return frozen funds, which is what the injunction is designed to satisfy. Binance has already said it replaced the affected assets with company funds, using part of its SAFU emergency fund and its corporate treasury, and aims to reopen all remaining networks and assets by the end of this week, per the same report.
