A connection is someone who recognizes your name. A professional relationship is someone who has enough experience with your work to know when to ask you for help, tell you when you are wrong, or trust you with a difficult task. Web3 has plenty of places to make connections. Durable relationships take more than appearing in the same Discord server, holding the same token, or exchanging a few messages during a conference.

The distinction matters for career growth because a referral, collaboration, mentorship, or co-founder conversation carries risk for both people. The other person is not doing you a favor by pretending not to see your gaps. They are deciding whether you communicate clearly, meet commitments, accept feedback, and treat people well when the work becomes awkward. You build that evidence in small interactions long before a job opening appears.

This guide concerns the quality and maintenance of those relationships. A separate networking practice can help you enter a community or make your work visible. Once you meet people, stop treating every interaction as a path to the next opportunity. Learn how to work with them.

## Build alongside people, not around them

Shared work gives a relationship a real object. It can be a pull request, an event, a research note, a governance proposal, a user interview, a bug report, a community call, or a small product experiment. The object makes expectations discussable. You can agree on scope, ask for review, notice a missed handoff, and acknowledge a useful contribution without inventing intimacy.

Choose work with a clear boundary. Instead of offering to "help with community," propose to organize a single onboarding session and write the follow-up notes. Instead of offering to "improve the protocol," reproduce one issue and submit the smallest safe fix. Instead of telling a mentor you want to learn DeFi, ask whether they would review a two-page analysis of one proposal after you have completed it. Bounded requests let both people decide what they can commit to.

Read the project's rules before joining its work. GitHub's [guide to open-source contributions](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/exploring-projects-on-github/contributing-to-open-source) identifies the README, contributing instructions, code of conduct, security policy, and issue process as sources of a project's expectations. In a healthy project, reading first is respect for maintainers and current contributors. It also keeps you from building an unrequested feature or exposing a security issue in the wrong place.

When you propose work, state the problem as you understand it, what you checked, the result you plan to deliver, and the question that needs an answer. For example: "The wallet onboarding page does not explain the network switch required before this transaction. I checked the current guide and support questions. I can draft a one-page change by Thursday if the team wants that scope." This is easier to trust than "I have ideas to make onboarding better."

Give credit precisely. Thank the person who reviewed the change, the person who found the original issue, and the person who wrote the reference material you used. Do not claim a team result as personal proof of work. Public accuracy about authorship is a practical sign that people can collaborate with you safely.

## Be specific when you ask for time

Many career relationships start with an ask. That is normal. The problem is making the other person discover what you need and how much time it will take.

Before asking for a call, try the work that would make the call useful. Read the documentation, watch the public talk, inspect the proposal, or run the example. The [Open Source Guides](https://opensource.guide/how-to-contribute/) recommend giving context, doing preliminary research, and keeping requests short because each request asks another contributor for review time. That advice applies equally to an engineer, researcher, community lead, or founder in Web3.

Ask one question that the person is well placed to answer. "I saw that your team changed its contributor compensation process. What did you wish new contributors understood before taking their first scoped task?" is a real question. "How do I break into Web3?" shifts all of the thinking to the recipient.

If you ask for feedback, make the artifact small and give the reviewer a lens. Say whether you want factual correction, a product critique, a code review, or advice on scope. State a deadline only if there is a real one. If they decline or do not respond, accept it without turning a public comment into pressure through direct messages. A respectful no leaves more room for a later, better-timed conversation than an entitled follow-up does.

After a useful exchange, report back once. Describe the action you took and the result. If the person suggested reading a specification and it changed your design, say what changed. If you decided not to follow their advice, say so plainly and explain the constraint if a response is appropriate. You are not required to agree with a mentor or collaborator. You are required to avoid pretending that their time disappeared into a void.

## Make feedback a normal part of the relationship

The fastest way to make someone cautious about working with you is to turn every review into a referendum on your ability. A review is a discussion about an artifact. Keep it there.

GitHub's [pull-request review documentation](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/collaborating-with-pull-requests/reviewing-changes-in-pull-requests/about-pull-request-reviews) describes three basic review outcomes: comments, approval, and requested changes. It also supports line comments and suggested changes. That structure offers a useful habit outside GitHub: name the part under discussion, state the concern, provide evidence or a concrete alternative, and decide what happens next.

When receiving feedback, first restate the concern in your own words. Ask a clarifying question if you cannot tell whether the issue is correctness, user impact, maintainability, risk, tone, or scope. Then make the change, explain why you are declining it, or ask for a decision from the accountable person. Silence is not an answer to a review. Neither is replying "fixed" when the change is not visible.

When giving feedback, do not use vague praise as a substitute for review. "Looks good" is pleasant but not very useful. Name the behavior or choice that helped: "The reproduction steps made the error easy to test," or "The proposal identifies who owns the migration." Pair criticism with enough context for the other person to act. "This is bad" does not meet that standard. "This path lets an uninitialized role perform the administrative action; can we add a test that fails until initialization runs?" does.

Keep criticism proportional. A typo, a disagreement over product direction, and an unsafe deployment do not deserve the same public temperature. Take sensitive feedback to the project's appropriate channel when public discussion would embarrass someone or reveal security details. Do not hide a material decision in a private message when the wider team needs the rationale.

## Treat conduct as part of professional skill

Web3 communities cross time zones, cultures, languages, experience levels, and identity choices. Misunderstandings happen. Harassment, exclusion, and public humiliation are different problems. A serious professional relationship needs a line between the two.

The [Contributor Covenant Code of Conduct](https://www.contributor-covenant.org/version/2/1/code_of_conduct/) asks participants to act in ways that support an open, welcoming, inclusive, and healthy community. Its examples include respectful disagreement, constructive feedback, responsibility for mistakes, and attention to the overall community rather than personal gain. You do not need a formal code of conduct to practice those behaviors. You do need to understand that technical skill does not excuse harmful conduct.

Do not force familiarity. A person may use a pseudonym, avoid video calls, keep personal information private, or prefer written communication. Respect the boundary without treating it as a defect to investigate. Ask what name and pronouns a person uses in the current space. Do not connect their accounts, address, employer, location, or legal identity without clear permission. In a field where public activity can be easy to trace, curiosity can become surveillance quickly.

If you make a mistake, address the behavior rather than making the affected person manage your guilt. State what you did, acknowledge the impact you now understand, apologize without demanding forgiveness, and change the behavior. If a community moderator gives a direction, follow it. Do not recruit friends to litigate the dispute in side channels.

When another person raises a concern about your work, resist the urge to decide their motives before checking the facts. Look at the artifact, logs, agreement, or message history. If the concern is valid, correct it. If you disagree, state the disagreement with evidence and use the defined decision process. This preserves working relationships even when the outcome is not what you wanted.

## Work carefully in DAO and pseudonymous settings

A DAO can make parts of coordination visible without turning strangers into trusted collaborators. Ethereum's [DAO overview](https://ethereum.org/en/dao/) describes collectively owned organizations that can use proposals and voting, smart-contract rules, and shared treasuries. It also describes token-, share-, and reputation-based membership models. Public proposals and on-chain votes can show that a decision happened. They do not prove that the proposal was well researched, that the vote was broadly understood, or that every contributor feels safe speaking in a chat channel.

Approach a DAO as a working organization with a specific governance model, not as a social identity. Read how proposals enter the process, who executes an approved action, how contributors are paid, which work is volunteer, and where conflicts are handled. If those answers are absent, do not assume that goodwill will fill the gap.

Pseudonymous collaboration works when you evaluate the work that a person chooses to show and avoid demanding information they have not offered. A contributor may demonstrate reliability through a series of reviewed changes, proposal comments, completed bounties, or agreed deliverables. Verify public claims where appropriate, especially when funds, access, or security are involved. Do not convert a wallet's visible activity into a sweeping story about the person behind it.

Start with small commitments. A new collaborator does not need control over a treasury, production signer, or critical deployment to prove they can help. Give a bounded task, define success, review the result, and increase responsibility only when the work supports it. This approach protects the project and gives the relationship an honest basis for growth.

Write agreements down. For paid work, clarify the deliverable, payment asset or currency, amount, payment timing, approver, ownership of work, and where disputes go. For volunteer work, clarify the expected time, public credit, and whether a contribution creates any governance right. Ambiguity is not decentralized. It is an invitation for the person with more power to reinterpret the arrangement later.

## Maintain relationships between projects

Relationships often fail after a project ends because people only know how to communicate through an active task. Do not manufacture check-ins to keep a contact warm. Reach out when you have a genuine reason: congratulate someone on a concrete release, ask a question related to their stated expertise, send an article that directly answers a problem they mentioned, or invite them to a small event they would plausibly enjoy.

Remember promises. If you said you would send a write-up, make the introduction, review the draft, or share the job listing, do it. If you cannot do it, say so before the deadline. A brief message such as "I will not finish the review today. I can return it Tuesday, or you should ask another reviewer" is far more useful than disappearing. Reliability is often visible in these ordinary handoffs.

Keep a modest private record if you need one: how you met, the public work you discussed, and any commitment you made. Do not record personal details that are irrelevant to the work. Do not assign people a private score or tally favors as if a relationship were a balance sheet. The record should help you avoid forgetting a promise, not make every interaction transactional.

Celebrate work accurately. If a collaborator gets hired, ships a release, wins a grant, or publishes research, congratulate them without implying you caused it. Share their work when you can explain why it is useful. The strongest support is often concrete: test the new release, report a bug clearly, attend the talk, introduce a qualified candidate with permission, or give feedback before a deadline.

## Handle strain before it becomes a story

Projects fail. Funding changes, a protocol is discontinued, a hackathon prototype goes nowhere, an application is rejected, or two collaborators discover they want incompatible things. The relationship test arrives after the exciting part ends.

Close a failed effort directly. State what was completed, what was not, where the files and credentials now live, what obligations remain, and who can reuse the work. Return access you no longer need. Pay invoices or explain the dispute process. Thank people for specific work. Do not vanish because the project did not become a success story.

When tension is rising, use the smallest channel that can resolve it. A misunderstood sentence may need one direct message. A product tradeoff may need a documented decision with the team. A conduct issue may need a moderator. Avoid turning every disagreement into a public thread, but do not use privacy to isolate someone or avoid accountability.

Name the next action at the end of a difficult conversation. "You will update the proposal, I will review the revised scope by Wednesday, and the steward will decide the budget question" is a workable end. "We should communicate better" is not.

Professional relationships in Web3 get stronger when people can see the same pattern over time: you show up prepared, make a clear commitment, respond to feedback, give credit, protect boundaries, and close work honestly. Send the follow-up you promised, then make the next shared artifact easier for someone else to understand.
