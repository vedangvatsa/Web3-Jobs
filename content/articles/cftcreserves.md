Staff at the Commodity Futures Trading Commission said on Nov. 23 that swap execution facilities, designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations may count bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins toward the financial resources they are required to hold. The [staff advisory](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9331-26) from the Division of Market Oversight and the Division of Clearing and Risk addresses the requirement that those venues keep resources covering at least one year of operating costs.

The advisory applies to three rules at once: Regulation 37.1301 for swap execution facilities, Regulation 38.1101(a) for designated contract markets and Regulation 39.11(a)(2) for derivatives clearing organizations, according to the [text of the advisory](https://www.cftc.gov/csl/26-31/download), issued as CFTC Staff Letter No. 26-31. The divisions wrote that crypto assets may serve as acceptable financial resources under those rules, "subject to appropriate haircuts."

The haircuts are specific. Staff said they would not object to a venue applying the capital charges that futures commission merchants use for their own positions in the same assets, which include a minimum 20% haircut for bitcoin and ether and a 2% haircut for payment stablecoins, [the advisory states](https://www.cftc.gov/csl/26-31/download). A crypto asset that is not readily convertible to cash would face a 100% haircut, leaving it unusable for the requirement. Staff also flagged wrong-way risk, meaning a period in which operational losses and falling crypto prices hit at the same time, as something a venue should weigh before relying on the assets.

The same advisory closes the other door. Because bitcoin, ether and stablecoins do not qualify as "cash" under generally accepted accounting principles and do not count as "highly liquid securities," they cannot be used for the separate liquidity requirement that sits on top of the operating-cost rule, [the advisory says](https://www.cftc.gov/csl/26-31/download). For swap execution facilities, that liquidity piece must be held in unencumbered liquid assets equal to at least the greater of three months of projected operating costs or the projected wind-down costs. Designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations both face a six-month operating-cost floor for the same requirement.

For the stablecoin bucket, staff drew a definition from the GENIUS Act. Before that law's effective date, a qualifying payment stablecoin is a dollar-denominated token issued by a state-regulated money transmitter, state-regulated trust company or national trust bank, holding reserves limited to cash, U.S. Treasury securities or overnight Treasury repurchase agreements and publishing monthly attestations on reserve composition, [the advisory explains](https://www.cftc.gov/csl/26-31/download). After the effective date, the GENIUS Act's own definition and its permitted issuer categories take over.

The advisory arrives as part of a longer run of staff work on crypto holdings at registered firms. The letter points to a December 2025 no-action position on digital assets accepted as margin collateral, later reissued as Staff Letter 26-05, a tokenized collateral guidance from the same month, and a set of crypto FAQs first posted in March 2026 and reissued with added questions as Release No. 9303-26 on Sept. 24, 2026. Venues also have to keep valuing their financial resources at regular intervals and applying haircuts as conditions change; clearinghouses must mark the positions at least monthly.

The guidance matters for a set of venues that now includes crypto-native firms. CFTC-regulated entities include Coinbase Derivatives, Crypto.com's North American Derivatives Exchange and Bitnomial Exchange, as well as prediction markets Kalshi and Polymarket US, [Cointelegraph reported](https://cointelegraph.com/news/cftc-staff-say-exchanges-can-count-bitcoin-toward-operating-reserves). Each of them must still cover the liquidity requirement with cash or highly liquid securities regardless of how much crypto sits in the operating-cost basket.

The advisory carries limits on its own weight. It represents the views of staff at the two divisions only, creates no new rules and confers no enforceable rights, and is not a no-action position, [the CFTC said in its release](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9331-26). It does not necessarily reflect the views of the Commission itself, and a formal rule change would require a separate vote by the five commissioners.

Registered entities had asked the divisions for an answer. The advisory responds to SEFs, DCMs and DCOs, along with applicants for designation or registration, that already hold crypto assets in connection with their businesses and wanted to know whether those holdings could count, [the letter says](https://www.cftc.gov/csl/26-31/download). The document is dated Nov. 23, 2026 and posted under Release Number 9331-26.
