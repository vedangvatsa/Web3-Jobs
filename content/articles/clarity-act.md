Sen. Cynthia Lummis, R-Wyo., released updated Senate text for the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act on Sept. 10, ahead of a Senate vote that her office said was expected on Sept. 15. The document is styled as an amendment in the nature of a substitute to H.R. 3633, meaning it would strike the House bill's text after the enacting clause and replace it if adopted. It is a proposal, not an enacted statute or a final agency rule. [Lummis's release](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/press-releases/lummis-releases-updated-clarity-act-text-ahead-of-historic-tuesday-vote-2/) identifies the scheduled vote and the update; the [official Senate substitute text](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) carries no named amendment number or calendar number on its first page.

The DeFi provisions are one part of a 630-page substitute. The draft also addresses the treatment and disclosure of certain "ancillary assets," digital-commodity intermediaries, stablecoins, banking activities, customer property in bankruptcy, ethics requirements, law-enforcement programs, and other subjects. Its table of contents divides the measure among a Banking Committee division, a Digital Commodity Intermediaries Act division, ethics provisions, and an effective-date division. [The substitute itself](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) calls the overall measure the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act and separately names its securities title the Lummis-Gillibrand Responsible Financial Innovation Act of 2026.

That scope differs substantially from the version the House sent to the Senate in 2025. The Government Publishing Office lists H.R. 3633 as an [Engrossed in House bill dated July 17, 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr3633eh), with the short title Digital Asset Market Clarity Act of 2025. The House text used a different structure and included an Anti-CBDC Surveillance State Act title. The Senate document would be a substitute for that House language, so descriptions of the House version do not by themselves establish what the Senate would vote on or what a final law would contain.

## The protocol test

The proposal does not treat every application called DeFi as a regulated entity. It first defines a "decentralized finance trading protocol" as a distributed-ledger system through which multiple participants can execute a financial transaction under a predetermined, nondiscretionary automated rule or algorithm, without relying on someone other than the user to hold custody or control of the digital assets in the transaction. [Section 10301 of the substitute](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) uses that definition for its securities-side rules, while Section 20209 repeats the relevant test for the commodities-side rules.

A protocol within that definition becomes a "non-decentralized finance trading protocol" if it meets at least one of three conditions. The first is that a person, or a group under common control or acting in concert, can directly or indirectly control or materially alter the protocol's functionality, operation, or consensus or agreement rules. The second is that the protocol does not operate, execute, and enforce its operations and transactions solely through pre-established, transparent rules encoded directly in the distributed-ledger system's source code. The third is that such a person or group can use the protocol to restrict, censor, or prohibit use of it, including system-based user activity. [The operative definition](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) is conjunctive only in the sense that a protocol must meet the base DeFi definition, then satisfy one or more of those three listed conditions.

Those words leave factual and legal questions for the agencies. The text refers to direct and indirect authority through a contract, arrangement, understanding, relationship, or otherwise, but it does not name particular protocols or prescribe a single technical test for determining control. It also directs the SEC, CFTC, and Treasury to construe the term consistently across the relevant provisions. A label, a governance token, or the use of open-source code would therefore not, on the text alone, answer whether a protocol or a person fits the definition. [Sections 10301 and 20209](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) require future rulemakings rather than supplying a completed list of covered services or controllers.

The draft gives decentralized governance systems a specific qualification. A transparent, rules-based system through which people form consensus on development, publication, maintenance, or administration can be a decentralized governance system when it is not under the unilateral control of a person or coordinated group. The substitute says the system and its participants must be treated as separate persons unless they are under common control or have an agreement, arrangement, or understanding to exercise control. It also says the governance system, solely because it operates as such, is not a person or group acting in concert. [The definitions and rule of construction](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) do not make every participant immune from a control finding; they set the treatment of the system and participants for the proposed rulemakings.

## Rules for controlling people

The proposal would instruct the SEC, in consultation with Treasury, to issue tailored rules after notice and comment. Those rules would clarify how a person or coordinated group that controls a defined non-decentralized DeFi protocol and is subject to the Securities Exchange Act must meet applicable requirements, including registration, conduct, disclosure, recordkeeping, supervision, and other securities-law requirements. The SEC's assessment must be activity-based and limited to securities-related functions performed by the controller, such as brokerage, dealing, trading, execution, clearing, or custody. [The securities provision](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) says the analysis must not turn on technological form, distributed architecture, or a claimed decentralized characterization.

Section 20209 would create a parallel CFTC process. The CFTC, consulting with the SEC and Treasury, would write rules explaining how covered controllers must comply with applicable Commodity Exchange Act requirements. Its activity-based test is directed to digital-commodity functions, including brokerage, dealing, trading, execution, clearing, and custody. [The CFTC provision](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) similarly calls for registration, conduct, disclosure, recordkeeping, supervision, and other requirements only as applicable under that Act.

The provisions do not say that each person associated with a protocol must register. Registration or registrant-like compliance follows only if the relevant agency determines it in the required rulemaking, and the applicable requirements are to be determined from the functions the controlling person or group performs. The draft also says that no presumption arises that a person or activity covered by either section is, or is not, subject to the Exchange Act, Commodity Exchange Act, or Bank Secrecy Act without that rulemaking determination. [The no-presumption clauses](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) are a limit on the proposal's immediate legal effect, not a declaration that existing law never applies.

Treasury's role is also narrower than a new, self-contained anti-money-laundering regime. For a controller that the SEC or CFTC determines must register or comply as a registrant, Treasury would define compliance with Bank Secrecy Act and related anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing duties through notice-and-comment rules. The text conditions that treatment on existing law causing the person to be treated as a financial institution because of the registration or compliance. [Both tracks of the substitute](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) say they neither expand nor contract the SEC's, CFTC's, or Treasury's statutory authority under the Bank Secrecy Act as it stood the day before enactment.

The draft preserves existing investigative and enforcement authorities for the SEC, CFTC, and Treasury regarding people determined through the new rulemakings to be covered. It does not itself settle how those agencies would exercise their existing authority in a particular investigation, and it does not state that a protocol's software becomes a financial institution. [The preservation and construction provisions](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) repeatedly distinguish a controller from a distributed-ledger system or software code.

## Code, interfaces, and spot-market limits

On the securities side, the text says neither the statute nor rules under its DeFi-controller section may require a distributed-ledger system or software code to register in its own capacity. It also says those provisions cannot be read to prohibit a system's launch, deployment, or operation. The corresponding CFTC section uses the same formulation for commodity regulation. [Sections 10301 and 20209](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) do not say that all conduct around software is exempt from regulation.

The proposal separately protects people from Commodity Exchange Act coverage solely because they compile, relay, search, sequence, or validate network transactions; provide computational work, node or oracle services, bandwidth, or similar incidental services; or develop, publish, or constitute a distributed-ledger system and certain wallet-related software. [The software-developer provision](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) expressly says that protection is based on the listed activity being the sole basis for coverage. It also says the broader developer language does not extend to certain post-deployment activities, which the statute addresses separately.

For decentralized finance trading protocols, the CFTC-side exclusion applies only to digital-commodity cash or spot-market regulatory provisions. It covers, when those activities alone are the basis for coverage, a user interface that lets a user read and access data; governance-system administration or distribution; administration or distribution of a DeFi messaging system; participation in a smart-contract liquidity pool; and certain wallet and custody software. [Section 4v proposed by Section 20209](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) therefore does not purport to exempt futures, swaps, or every other category of activity from all federal law.

The same provision retains the CFTC's anti-fraud, anti-manipulation, and false-reporting enforcement authorities. Its state-law preemption for the specified software and spot-market activities likewise excludes state anti-money-laundering, anti-fraud, and anti-manipulation authority. It says the provision does not grant new CFTC jurisdiction or expand the agency's pre-enactment authority. [Those savings clauses](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) matter because the listed exclusions are limited by their own conditions and exceptions.

The substitute also addresses web-hosted "distributed ledger messaging systems," defined as applications that let a user create or submit instructions or messages to a ledger application or DeFi trading protocol to execute a transaction. The definition excludes a ledger application, protocol, or system; a DeFi trading protocol; clients, nodes, validators, and other computational infrastructure; and software or hardware wallets. Treasury would have 360 days after enactment to issue guidance for U.S.-owned or operated messaging systems on sanctions and illicit-finance obligations, risk management, and compliance considerations. [Section 10302](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) says that guidance could include address screening and measures to block or restrict prohibited or high-risk transactions, while also stating that it does not expand or contract existing sanctions, anti-money-laundering, or financial-institution definitions.

## Emergency powers and timing

Participation in an incident-response or security council would not by itself establish control under the proposal. That exception is conditional. The council's action must be a pre-defined, temporary, rules-based cybersecurity measure responding exclusively to a specific and documented incident or imminent threat; it must use publicly disclosed on-chain authorization; it must be limited in scope and duration; and no one person can hold unilateral control. The procedures and operational limits must be in public written documentation reasonably available to the relevant federal regulator before those emergency powers are used. [The emergency-measure language](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) says such measures cannot be used for unrelated protocol upgrades, governance decisions, or economic changes.

The legislation would generally take effect 360 days after enactment. A provision requiring rulemaking would take effect later: 360 days after enactment or 60 days after the final implementing rule is published in the Federal Register. [The effective-date section](https://www.lummis.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/EHF26718.pdf) reinforces that the DeFi-controller provisions would not create an immediate registration duty if the Senate adopts the substitute and the bill later becomes law.
