Web3 teams often say that hiring is hard. The phrase can hide several different problems. A protocol may need an engineer who can work safely with smart contracts. A startup may need a product manager who can turn unfamiliar technical constraints into sensible product choices. A DAO may need someone who can run grants, governance processes, contributor payments, or community operations with clear ownership. A candidate may have strong experience in a related field but no public proof that they understand the specific environment.

Treating all of that as a shortage of "crypto-native" talent makes the problem harder to solve. Teams need to identify the actual work, the risks attached to it, and the evidence that would show a candidate can do it. Candidates need routes to gain that evidence without first winning a role that requires it. Education, well-scoped contributions, better hiring criteria, and credible operating practices can widen the pool on both sides.

This is part of a larger skills problem. The [World Economic Forum's 2025 Future of Jobs report](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/digest/) says 63% of surveyed employers identified skills gaps as a major barrier to business transformation over the following five years. That survey covers many industries and should not be read as a measurement of Web3 hiring. It does show that teams competing for technical and operational talent need an explicit development strategy rather than an assumption that the right applicants will appear.

### The Work Is More Specific Than a Job Title

Many Web3 job descriptions bundle several jobs into one. A posting for a community lead may ask for content, moderation, events, support, token knowledge, partnership work, and analytics. A posting for a protocol engineer may ask for Solidity, frontend experience, infrastructure knowledge, security judgment, and the ability to explain tradeoffs to non-engineers. A candidate cannot prepare well for a role that has no clear center of gravity.

Start with the work that must be done in the first six months. Write down the systems the person will touch, decisions they will own, people they will work with, and outcomes that will show the work is going well. Separate required experience from skills that can be learned on the job. If an engineer must review upgradeable contracts in the first month, that is a real requirement. If the team would merely prefer someone who has used every tool in its stack, say so instead of treating it as mandatory.

Clear scope also protects candidates. It lets them decide whether the work matches their ability and appetite for risk. It lets a hiring manager compare applicants against the same criteria. Most of all, it gives the new hire a usable definition of success after they join.

### The Technical Learning Curve Is Real

Developers moving into Web3 need more than a new programming language. Solidity's [introduction to smart contracts](https://docs.soliditylang.org/en/latest/introduction-to-smart-contracts.html) describes a contract as code and state at a particular address on Ethereum, explains the Ethereum Virtual Machine as the runtime environment for smart contracts, and covers concepts such as transactions, gas, storage, message calls, and events. Those concepts affect design choices. A developer who understands only syntax may still miss how a contract is executed, what a transaction costs, or how data persists.

Security raises the bar further. Ethereum.org's [smart-contract security guidance](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/smart-contracts/security/) says deployed contract code usually cannot be changed to patch a flaw and recommends extensive testing, independent review, and practices such as version control and pull-request review. That does not mean every Web3 developer must be a security auditor. It does mean teams should avoid treating smart-contract work as ordinary application work with a different API.

The learning curve is not solely technical. A product manager may need to understand what actions happen onchain, which actions are irreversible, where a wallet is required, how a transaction fails, and what a user can recover. A support lead may need a careful process for dealing with wallet and transaction questions without asking users for secret credentials. A writer may need to distinguish a protocol feature from a marketing claim. Each role has subject-matter learning to do, even when it does not write code.

Teams can make this learning visible. Publish an architecture overview, a glossary in the team's own language, a contribution guide, and examples of good decisions. Explain the problem a system solves before pointing newcomers to a repository. Good documentation will not turn every applicant into a specialist. It will reduce time spent decoding undocumented assumptions.

### The Crypto-Native Requirement Can Become a Closed Door

Prior Web3 experience can be useful. It may show familiarity with wallets, public repositories, open communities, token mechanics, or a particular protocol family. It should not become a shortcut for every requirement. "Crypto-native" is too vague to evaluate fairly. One candidate may have spent years trading tokens and never shipped a product. Another may have built secure distributed systems, run a large community, or managed financial operations without calling themselves crypto-native.

Replace the label with observable requirements. For example:

- "Can read a Solidity contract and explain the security assumptions to the product team."
- "Has managed an online community with clear moderation and escalation practices."
- "Can produce accurate technical content after interviewing engineers and checking primary documentation."
- "Can run a contributor-payment process with documented approvals and records."

Each statement helps a candidate decide whether to apply and helps a reviewer know what evidence to request. It also reveals which requirements can be tested through a paid project, work sample, reference, or probationary scope.

Hiring for aptitude still requires rigor. A candidate's curiosity, reasoning, and willingness to learn are valuable only when the role has support, boundaries, and a realistic ramp. Do not hire someone into a security-critical position based on enthusiasm alone. Do give strong candidates a route to demonstrate adjacent skills through a limited assignment, a code review discussion, a writing exercise, or a project that reflects the real work.

### Volatility and Trust Affect Career Decisions

Candidates assess more than salary when they consider a Web3 role. They may ask whether the company has a clear legal entity, how it pays people, what portion of compensation is cash or tokens, who controls a treasury, whether employment is remote across borders, and what happens if the project's priorities change. Those are reasonable questions. A team that refuses to answer them can lose capable people before the technical interview.

Be plain about what you know and do not know. Explain compensation structure, vesting, benefits where applicable, payment currency, employment classification, location expectations, and reporting line before the final stage. Do not sell token upside as guaranteed compensation. Do not describe a volunteer contributor role as a job. Do not imply that a DAO's governance process is a substitute for a manager, a payroll process, or a decision maker when the work requires those things.

Trust also comes from operating discipline. Candidates notice whether a team has written priorities, clear decision rights, basic security practices, and a respectful interview process. Those are not cosmetic details. They shape the daily work and the candidate's ability to judge whether they can succeed.

### Invest in Education and Onboarding

The industry needs material for people at different starting points. Developers need primary documentation, runnable examples, test environments, security training, and feedback on code. Product, design, marketing, operations, legal, and community candidates need an explanation of the technology and the business model that does not assume years of prior immersion.

Documentation deserves an owner. The Solidity documentation provides a detailed example of how much conceptual groundwork a newcomer may need before they can reason about a contract. A team does not need to reproduce a language manual, but it should explain its architecture, terminology, user flows, integration points, and operational rules. When a contributor must discover this information through scattered chat messages, onboarding becomes dependent on who happens to be online.

Create role-specific onboarding paths. A developer's first weeks might include setting up the development environment, reading the threat model, running tests, fixing a documentation issue, and pairing on a small change. A product manager's path might include using the product, mapping a user flow, reading prior decisions, and speaking with support or community members. A community operator's path might include moderation rules, escalation routes, tooling access, and a sample response review.

Do not confuse a pile of links with onboarding. A new person needs sequence, context, a person who can answer questions, and a first contribution that is small enough to complete. End each stage with a check: what should this person now understand, perform, or decide? If nobody can answer that question, the onboarding material needs work.

Independent educators can also help candidates enter the field. Teams can support them by publishing accurate public material, responding to technical questions, and making grants or sponsorships transparent when they fund education. The boundary matters. Education should explain the technology and its limits, not disguise promotion as neutral instruction.

### Create Clear Paths to Contribution

People learn by doing work that someone else can inspect. A well-designed contribution path creates that opportunity. It might be a documentation correction, test coverage, localization, a research brief, a design review, a support playbook, a data-quality task, or a small product improvement. The best first tasks have a clear owner, written acceptance criteria, accessible context, and feedback at the end.

Bounty programs can provide a first work sample when they are structured well. A vague bounty with unclear scope, changing requirements, and delayed payment teaches newcomers to avoid the project. Before offering one, define the deliverable, eligibility, review process, reward, payment timing, intellectual-property terms, and contact person. Say whether multiple people can submit work and what happens if no submission meets the standard.

Pay promptly for accepted work. If a task is exploratory or unpaid, label it honestly and keep the expected effort low. Projects should not use endless unpaid test tasks as a hiring funnel. A paid, time-bounded exercise that reflects the role is clearer and fairer for both sides.

Mentorship can turn a one-off task into a development path. Pairing a newcomer with an experienced contributor is useful when both people know the purpose. The experienced person can explain the system, review a draft, identify next skills, and make the quality bar visible. The newcomer should bring preparation, document questions, and complete the agreed work. Neither side benefits from an arrangement with no scope or schedule.

The [Electric Capital Developer Report](https://www.developerreport.com/) publishes an open data project and tracks monthly active developers in crypto ecosystems. Its dashboard is a reminder that open-source activity leaves public traces. Candidates can build credible evidence through reviewed contributions, issue discussions, documentation, reproducible projects, and technical writing. They do not need a famous employer to show how they work.

### Hire for Evidence and Learning Capacity

Recruiters and hiring managers should review the work before filtering on a pedigree. Ask candidates to explain a project, a decision, a tradeoff, an error, and how they would approach a relevant problem. For a technical role, the discussion may cover a repository, code sample, architecture exercise, or code review. For an operations role, it may cover a process map and escalation plan. For a communications role, it may cover a technical brief rewritten for a defined audience.

Build a scorecard before interviews begin. List the capabilities required for the role, the evidence that will demonstrate each one, and who will assess it. Keep the scorecard short enough to use. If a requirement cannot be defined or assessed, decide whether it is truly a requirement.

Give candidates context for any exercise. A task that depends on private system knowledge measures whether the candidate can guess, not whether they can do the job. State the goal, constraints, time expectation, and evaluation criteria. Pay candidates when the task produces work the organization can use or takes substantial time. Do not ask for a production-ready solution as a free audition.

An immersion period can help a candidate new to Web3, but it should be planned rather than treated as a month of self-directed reading. Set the topics, people to meet, systems to use, first deliverable, and checkpoints. A person entering product operations may need a different immersion plan from a smart-contract engineer. The role determines the curriculum.

### Professionalize DAO Operations

DAOs can involve contributors working across time zones, organizations, and different levels of commitment. That flexibility does not remove the need for operating basics. People need to know who can make a decision, how work is assigned, how payment is approved, where records live, and how disputes are handled.

Define roles in terms of work and authority. A governance facilitator, grants operator, community lead, treasury contributor, and protocol engineer may all participate in one DAO, but they should not be expected to infer their responsibilities from chat history. Publish the scope, expected time commitment, reporting route, and compensation terms. Revise them when the organization changes.

Compensation needs particular care. State whether a role is employment, a contract, a stipend, a bounty, or volunteer work. Explain the payment process and timing. If compensation includes tokens, explain the amount, conditions, vesting if any, and risks without pretending that its value is fixed. Candidates will make better decisions when the offer is written in terms they can compare.

Career progression also needs definition. A contributor may want to know how they move from a small task to a larger grant, a recurring contract, or a leadership responsibility. Create criteria based on reliable work, quality, collaboration, and demonstrated judgment. Avoid private ladders that only long-standing insiders can navigate.

### What Teams Can Do This Quarter

Pick one role that has been difficult to fill. Rewrite the job description around first-six-month work and observable evidence. Remove vague requirements. Identify which skills must be present on day one and which the team can teach.

Then inspect onboarding. Ask a recent hire or contributor to list the questions they had in their first month. Turn repeated answers into documentation, examples, and a sequence. Give the material an owner and a review date.

Finally, create one paid, limited contribution path for newcomers. It should be real work, reviewed by a named person, and connected to the role you want to develop. The aim is not to generate a large volume of applications. It is to give capable people a fair chance to produce evidence.

Closing the Web3 talent gap will take repeated work by projects, DAOs, educators, and candidates. The practical standard is simple: define the work, teach what is teachable, pay for real contributions, and judge people by evidence that relates to the role.
