The Czech National Bank announced on November 10 that it had issued its own bonds in tokenized form for the first time and completed a round of tests on distributed-ledger infrastructure, covering bond transfers, purchases and sales against payment, and repo transactions carried out during October and November. The [press release](https://www.cnb.cz/en/cnb-news/press-releases/CNB-issues-its-first-DLT-bond/) said settlement took place almost instantaneously and that transaction volume was in the tens of thousands of koruna.

The bonds were recorded on the DLT infrastructure operated by the Central Securities Depository, which also registers and settles trades in the securities. A domestic commercial bank acted as counterparty for the tests. [CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/czech-central-bank-tests-dlt-bonds-boxing-out-stablecoins/) reported that Governor Ales Michl named Ceska sporitelna as that counterparty in a November 10 speech, put each bond's nominal value at 10,000 koruna and said interest payments were also tested. [TokenPost](https://www.tokenpost.com/news/technology/57639) put the face value at 10,000 koruna, about $453, and reported that the bonds ran on CSD Prague's platform, the first infrastructure licensed under the European Union's DLT Pilot Regime.

Access to the network is restricted. The bank said only approved financial institutions can join it and that transaction records stay visible only to the parties involved.

The bank said the work showed it can use the new market infrastructure both for conducting monetary policy operations and for managing its international reserves, roles that would put tokenized securities into the ordinary machinery of the central bank rather than into a standalone experiment.

Michl framed the test as preparation rather than a completed overhaul. "We are conservative in monetary policy and innovative in everything else," he said in the release. "We started with instant payments, thanks to which money now moves instantly from one account to another. Now we have tested the next level: DLT. A possible platform for the future." He also set out a market objective: "The goal is clear: to create a large market for koruna-denominated assets that are easier to buy and sell than German Bunds." The central bank, he added, has to be involved from the outset so that settlement rests on central bank money instead of private money such as stablecoins.

That last clause is the part of the announcement that reaches beyond the Czech market. If eligible institutions can settle tokenized securities trades through central-bank money infrastructure, CryptoSlate noted, those transactions do not need to generate demand for stablecoins as a settlement asset. The implication stays forward-looking: the CNB has not disclosed which cash instrument actually handled the payment leg during the pilot, so no stablecoin displacement has been measured.

The timing of the cash arrangements is still unclear. Five days before the bond announcement, a November 5 presentation by CNB payments-regulation director Michal Vodrazka stated that settlement in CNB money on DLT was not yet available, according to CryptoSlate's reading of the document. The same presentation said the CNB Board had made no decision to issue a Czech retail central bank digital currency. The November 10 release did not say whether the cash setup had changed by then.

The bank is running parallel tokenization work in its reserves. The press release said the CNB recently invested in a euro-denominated government bond that exists exclusively on DLT, as a practical test for holding such instruments in international reserves. A year earlier, the CNB Lab innovation hub had started a digital asset test portfolio containing bitcoin, a stablecoin and tokenized deposits, work that Michl's release described as the predecessor to the bond project. In the pilot, the bank said, it walked through the whole chain, from changes to contracts and rules through transaction execution and settlement to portfolio management, and that the experience raises its readiness for broader use of tokenized securities.

Michl's ambition for a deep koruna market sits ahead of what the test shows. CryptoSlate judged the easier trading versus German Bunds to be a stated goal rather than a demonstrated result, since the pilot moved only tens of thousands of koruna through one named counterparty.

For comparison, CryptoSlate pointed to the Eurosystem's Pontes service, launched September 21 to let wholesale tokenized assets settle in central bank money, with the ECB planning significantly longer operating hours by mid-2027 and continuous availability in 2028.

The Czech tests are small in size. Volumes stayed in the tens of thousands of koruna, one named bank served as counterparty, and the payment leg remains undisclosed, facts the release itself sets out plainly.
