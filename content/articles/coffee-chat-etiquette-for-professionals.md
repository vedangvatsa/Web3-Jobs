## The Purpose of a Coffee Chat

A coffee chat is an informal professional conversation, held in person or by video, where one person learns from another person's experience. The name is casual. The request still asks someone to give you time, attention, and advice. Treat it with the care you would bring to any short meeting.

The purpose is information and connection, not an immediate job offer. UC Berkeley Career Engagement describes an [informational interview](https://career.berkeley.edu/start-exploring/informational-interviews/) as an informal conversation, usually lasting twenty to thirty minutes, with someone working in a field that interests you. Its guidance is clear that the conversation is not a job interview and should not be framed as a direct request for openings. That distinction makes coffee chats more useful. You can ask about the work, the route into it, and the tradeoffs without forcing the other person into a hiring conversation.

Use a coffee chat when you have a real question. You may be comparing roles, trying to understand an industry, considering a move, preparing for a job search, or learning how an organization works. Do not request a meeting because you believe every networking interaction must lead to a referral. A good conversation can give you a clearer next step even if it never produces an introduction.

The etiquette is straightforward: ask with a reason, prepare enough to avoid wasting their time, listen closely, end when promised, and follow up with a sincere note. The rest of this guide shows how to do each part without sounding overly formal or treating people as a means to an end.

## Part 1: Make a Respectful Request

Start with people you have a legitimate reason to contact. That could be an alumnus from your school, a former colleague, a person introduced by someone you know, a speaker whose work you heard, or a practitioner whose public work answered part of your question. You do not need a close connection, but you should be able to state why you chose them.

Research enough to make the request personal. Read their profile, recent writing, talk description, open-source contribution, or company bio. Find one genuine connection to your question. Do not pretend you have followed their work for years if you found it yesterday. A short, honest sentence is better.

UC Berkeley's [outreach guidance](https://career.berkeley.edu/prepare-for-success/networking/5-tips-for-reaching-out/) recommends brief, tailored messages that state the connection, explain why you want to talk, and request a conversation rather than a job. Use that structure. People can decide quickly when the request is clear.

### Write a Subject Line That Says What You Need

For email, use a subject line that identifies the context without trying to manufacture urgency. Good options include:

- `Question about your transition into product operations`
- `Introduced by Maya Patel`
- `Request for a 20-minute conversation about protocol design`
- `Your article on community moderation`

Avoid subject lines such as `Quick question` when you are asking for a meeting. The recipient may have many quick questions waiting in their inbox. State enough context that they can decide whether they are the right person to help.

For a LinkedIn connection request, you have less space. State who you are, the connection, and the reason for contacting them. Do not send a blank invitation if a short note would explain the request.

### Give the Person a Reason to Say Yes

Your message needs four things: a brief introduction, the specific reason you chose them, the question you want to explore, and a small time request. You can say that you will work around their schedule, but do not ask them to solve scheduling without giving a boundary.

Here is a simple email:

> Subject: Question about your move from support to product
>
> Hi Jordan,
>
> I am a customer support specialist exploring product operations roles. I found your post about moving from support into product at an early-stage company, and I was interested in the way you described using support patterns to prioritize work.
>
> Would you be open to a 20-minute video call in the next two weeks? I would like to ask about the skills that helped you make the move and the work you wish you had seen earlier. I am happy to work around your schedule.
>
> Thank you,
> Sam

The message is short because it has one purpose. It does not attach a resume, ask for a referral, or include a life story. If the person agrees, you will have time to provide context during the call.

If you share a mutual contact, ask that person first whether they are comfortable with an introduction. Give them a short, forwardable paragraph about who you are and why you want to connect. An introduction is easier to make when the other person can forward a prepared note without editing it.

### Choose a Reasonable Time Request

Ask for twenty minutes or thirty minutes, and mean it. A short meeting lowers the commitment and forces you to prepare. If the conversation runs longer because the other person chooses to continue, that is their decision. Do not book an hour for a first coffee chat unless they suggest it.

Offer a few time windows if you have access to their preferred scheduling method. If they provide a calendar link, choose an open slot and confirm promptly. If they do not reply, send one polite follow-up after about a week. Keep it to two sentences. Do not send a sequence of reminders or ask a mutual contact to pressure them.

No response is an answer. People may be busy, overwhelmed, traveling, or uninterested. Move on without resentment. A respectful request does not create an obligation.

## Part 2: Prepare to Lead the Conversation

The person you contacted should not have to invent the meeting's agenda. Prepare enough to ask informed questions, then leave room for their experience to take the conversation somewhere useful.

Begin with a short introduction of your own. Aim for thirty seconds: who you are, what you have done that is relevant, what you are exploring, and what you hope to learn. UC Berkeley's [networking guidance](https://career.berkeley.edu/prepare-for-success/networking/) suggests being ready to say what you care about, what you can do, what kind of work you want, and what you want to know. You do not need to recite all of that. It is a useful framework for getting your own story straight.

For example:

> I have spent the last two years in customer support for a developer tool. I enjoy finding repeated problems and working with product and engineering to address them. I am now exploring product operations, and I wanted to understand how that work differs between a small startup and a larger company.

That introduction tells the other person where to begin. It also prevents the common mistake of spending ten minutes explaining your entire resume before asking a question.

### Research the Person and Their Context

Read the basics before the meeting. Look at their current role, prior roles, public projects, writing, and the product or organization they work for. You are not preparing a biography. You are looking for context that will make the conversation more specific.

If they work at a company you are considering, read the company's website, product documentation, recent announcements, and job listings. If they work in Web3, try the product where appropriate, read its documentation, and understand enough terminology to ask a useful question. Do not ask them to explain information available on the first page of their site.

Research also helps you avoid awkward assumptions. A title such as product manager, community lead, researcher, or engineer can mean different work at different organizations. Ask how their role actually fits into the team rather than assuming you already know.

### Prepare Questions That Need Experience to Answer

Bring five to seven questions. You may only use three. The list keeps you from freezing, but it should not become an interrogation. Ask follow-up questions when the answer is interesting.

UC Berkeley's [question guide](https://career.berkeley.edu/start-exploring/informational-interviews/questions/) includes questions about responsibilities, decisions, career history, preparation, and related fields. Adapt those categories to the person in front of you. Good questions invite a story or judgment that search results cannot provide.

Try questions such as:

- What does a normal week look like in your role when things are going well?
- Which decisions do you own, and which decisions need agreement from other teams?
- What problem takes more of your time than people outside the role expect?
- What skills have become more useful as you moved into this work?
- I noticed you moved from [earlier role] to [current role]. What changed in the work you did day to day?
- What would you want to see in a candidate who is trying to enter this field?
- Which adjacent roles should I look at if I enjoy this part of the work?
- Is there anything you wish you had known before taking this job?

Avoid questions that ask for confidential information, personal salary details, or internal drama. You can ask about salary ranges published for a field, but do not pressure someone to disclose their own pay. You can ask about culture, but do not ask them to gossip about colleagues. Your goal is to learn how to make a better career decision, not to collect private details.

## Part 3: Conduct the Meeting Well

Confirm the meeting after it is scheduled. Send a calendar invitation with a clear title, the correct time zone, video link if needed, and your contact information. For an in-person meeting, confirm the location and offer to meet there. If you chose the venue, pick a place that is easy to find and quiet enough to talk.

Arrive early. For a video call, test your camera, microphone, lighting, and connection. Open your notes and close distractions. For an in-person meeting, arrive a few minutes before the agreed time and buy your own drink. You can offer to buy theirs, but do not argue if they decline or prefer to pay for themselves.

### Open With Purpose and Gratitude

Start by thanking them and restating the time boundary. Then give your short introduction and begin with the question that led you to request the chat.

> Thank you for making time. I know we have twenty minutes, and I will keep us to that. I work in customer support for a developer tool and am exploring product operations. I was hoping to learn how you made that transition and what your role looks like now.

This opening removes uncertainty. The other person knows why they are there and how long the meeting will take. It also gives them permission to be direct if they need to leave on time.

### Listen More Than You Speak

The conversation should center on their perspective. That does not mean you should be silent. Share relevant context when it helps them give a better answer. Then return to a question or follow-up.

Listen for specifics: the first project they owned, the skills they had to build, the people they worked with, the mistakes they made, and how they judged a good outcome. Write brief notes if you can do so without breaking eye contact. If you are on video, say that you are taking notes so the person does not wonder why you are looking away.

Do not try to prove that you already know everything. If they use a term you do not understand, ask. If you disagree with their advice, you can explore the difference without turning the chat into a debate. "That is helpful. I have seen a different approach in support work. How would you think about that in your context?" keeps the conversation curious and professional.

Do not pitch yourself for most of the meeting. A coffee chat can lead to a referral later, but an immediate sales pitch puts pressure on someone who agreed to share information. Let them know your interests through your introduction and questions. If they ask for your resume or offer to introduce you, send the material after the meeting.

### Ask for an Introduction Carefully

Near the end, you can ask whether there is another person whose perspective would help. Phrase it so they can decline easily:

> Is there anyone else you think I should speak with to understand this work from another angle? No pressure at all. I appreciate the time you have already given me.

If they name someone, ask whether you may use their name when reaching out. If they offer a direct introduction, send a short forwardable note within a day. Include your name, brief background, the reason you want to speak, and the requested meeting length. Do not make the introducer write your pitch for you.

An introduction is not a referral. Do not treat it as an endorsement or assume the next person has time. Make a new, respectful request and prepare again.

### End on Time

Watch the clock. Two or three minutes before the end, signal that you are wrapping up. Ask one final question if it fits, thank them, and leave the meeting when promised.

> We are close to the end of our time, so I want to respect your schedule. One last question: if you were in my position, what would you do next? Thank you again for the advice.

Ending on time is part of the meeting, not a detail. It shows that your request for twenty minutes was real. If they continue the conversation, follow their lead. If they need to leave, let them go without trying to add one more question at the door.

## Part 4: Follow Up With Care

Send a thank-you message within a day or two. Mention one specific point from the conversation and say how you plan to use it. This shows that you listened and gives the message a reason to exist beyond ritual.

For example:

> Hi Jordan,
>
> Thank you again for speaking with me today. Your point about learning the support workflow before trying to change it gave me a useful way to approach my transition. I am going to map the three issues I see most often and talk with our product operations partner about how they are tracked.
>
> I appreciate your time and advice.
>
> Sam

Keep the first thank-you note free of new demands. If you promised a resume, portfolio link, article, or short introduction blurb, include it. Otherwise, do not turn gratitude into a second request before the person has had a chance to close the first interaction.

### Keep in Touch Only When You Have a Reason

One coffee chat does not create a close professional relationship. It can begin one. The most respectful way to stay in touch is to provide a relevant update, acknowledge a real milestone, or share a resource that is clearly connected to a prior conversation.

If you followed their advice, tell them what happened. If they recommended a book, event, or person, thank them after you use the recommendation. If you read something that relates directly to a question you discussed, send a short note explaining why you thought of them. Do not forward a stream of generic articles, congratulate them on every social post, or write only when you need a job lead.

Give the relationship room. Some people enjoy regular contact; others will be helpful once and then remain a distant connection. Both are fine. You are building a reputation for being thoughtful, prepared, and respectful of time, not trying to force familiarity.

## Virtual and In-Person Details

Virtual chats need the same care as in-person meetings. Use a clear display name, reliable audio, and a neutral background if you can. Wear something appropriate for the field and the person you are meeting. You do not need formal interview clothes for every call, but you should look ready for a professional conversation.

Do not record a call without explicit permission. Do not take screenshots for social media. Do not share details from the conversation with others unless the person has said you can. People may be more candid in a private chat because they expect discretion.

For in-person meetings, respect the place and the person's comfort. Offer a public, convenient venue. If they prefer a video call, do not push for coffee. If they arrive late, stay gracious and use the remaining time well. If you are delayed, message them as soon as you know and offer to reschedule rather than assuming they can wait.

## Common Coffee Chat Mistakes

Asking for a job in the opening message changes the nature of the request. You can be transparent that you are exploring a field or starting a job search. Ask first for information and perspective. If the conversation later creates a reason to discuss an opening, let that happen naturally.

Showing up unprepared wastes the other person's generosity. You do not need to know their full career history, but you should know their current role and have questions that go beyond a search result.

Talking too much prevents you from learning. Your background matters only when it helps them answer. Give a short version, then listen for the parts of their experience that you cannot get elsewhere.

Ignoring the scheduled end time is another avoidable error. A strong conversation can still leave a poor impression if you treat the person's next meeting as optional. Close on time and let them decide whether to continue.

Treating an introduction as an entitlement also damages trust. Thank people who help, make their job easy, and accept that they may decline. The same rule applies when you eventually ask for a referral: ask only after you have enough information to know the role fits and enough relationship to make the request reasonable.

## A Simple Checklist

Before the request, identify the person, the specific reason to contact them, and the question you need help answering. Keep the message brief and ask for a short meeting.

Before the chat, research their role and organization, prepare a short introduction, and write a small set of open questions. Test your technology or confirm the location.

During the chat, thank them, state the time boundary, listen closely, ask follow-up questions, and wrap up on time. Ask for another perspective only if it fits the conversation.

After the chat, send a short thank-you note that refers to a real point from the discussion. Keep in touch when you have a genuine update or useful reason to do so.

A professional coffee chat works because both people know what it is for. You bring preparation and curiosity. The other person shares experience within a clear time boundary. Treat that exchange with care, and the conversation can remain useful long after the coffee is finished.
