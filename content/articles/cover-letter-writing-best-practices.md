A cover letter gives the reader the connection that a [resume](/how-to-build-a-web3-resume-that-stands-out) cannot show by itself. The resume records roles, dates, skills, and outcomes. The letter selects a small number of those facts and explains why they fit this specific position. The University of Washington Career & Internship Center frames the task as answering two questions: why this position interests you and why you are a strong fit for it [UW Career Center](https://careers.uw.edu/resources/102733/).

That is a narrow job. A cover letter does not need to tell your life story, repeat every bullet on your resume, or prove that you know every detail about a company. It needs to make a credible case for a conversation. Write it after you have read the role description closely and before you have sent the same application to another employer. The best letters are specific because the writer made choices about what to include.

### 1. Address It to a Specific Person

Use a named recipient when you can confirm that person is involved in the hiring process. The job posting, company website, professional network, or a referral may identify the recruiter, hiring manager, or department leader. Check the spelling, title, and pronouns before using a name. An inaccurate greeting creates more friction than a neutral one.

If you cannot identify the right person, use a direct professional greeting such as "Dear Hiring Team" or "Dear [Company] Recruiting Team." Do not spend an hour guessing from an organization chart. The substance of the letter carries more weight than a forced salutation. Avoid "To Whom It May Concern," which sounds detached, and do not address the letter to an executive who is unlikely to read it.

Personalization should continue after the greeting. Mention a product, role responsibility, recent public work, or company objective only if you can connect it to your experience. A sentence copied from an About page does not establish interest. A sentence that names a problem you have worked on and explains why the position is a fit does.

### 2. Start with a Strong Opening

The opening paragraph should state the role and give the reader a reason to continue. You do not need a dramatic hook. A precise opening is more convincing than a broad declaration of passion. Name the position, then offer one relevant credential, outcome, or connection. The reader should understand within a few lines what you do and why you are applying.

Avoid starting with: "I am writing to apply for the [Job Title] position I saw on [Platform]." The reader already has the application and knows why you are writing. Replace it with a sentence that makes a claim you can support in the body.

Here are three useful approaches:

- **Lead with a relevant achievement.** "In my last growth role, I rebuilt the content intake process and reduced the time from request to published campaign from ten days to four. I am applying for the Content Operations Manager role because your team is building a similar process across product launches."
- **Name a grounded company connection.** "After using [Company]'s developer documentation to integrate [feature], I was interested in the Developer Advocate opening. My recent work teaching API implementation to customer engineering teams would let me contribute to the same audience."
- **Mention a real referral.** "[Name], who worked with me on [project], encouraged me to apply for the Senior Engineer role. We shipped [specific outcome] together, and the platform work described in this position closely matches the systems I have been maintaining."

Do not make an achievement vague to sound impressive. "Increased organic traffic significantly" leaves the reader unable to judge the work. Give the actual measure if you can share it: a percentage, time period, budget range, adoption result, or operational result. If the result is confidential, describe the scope without inventing precision. "Led the launch content for a regulated product across three markets" is clearer than "drove major results."

### 3. Connect Your Experience to the Job Description

Most letters improve when the writer chooses two or three requirements from the job description and builds the body around them. Read the description with a pen or a note document. Mark the responsibilities that recur, the skills described as required, the outcomes the role owns, and the tools or subject matter that appear central. Then compare those points with evidence from your own work.

Do not mirror the job description word for word. Translate it into a claim about your experience. If the job asks for stakeholder management, choose an example where you aligned people with conflicting priorities. If it asks for data analysis, choose work where the analysis changed a decision. If it asks for community management, choose an example that shows how you handled feedback, moderation, or a recurring program. The letter should make the bridge visible so the reader does not have to infer it.

The STAR method can help you keep each example focused: Situation, Task, Action, and Result. It is a structure, not a script. Give only enough situation for the reader to understand the constraint. State the task you personally owned. Describe the action in active language. End with the outcome, including what changed and how it was measured when possible.

For example: "When a partner onboarding program was missing its target, I was asked to identify the bottleneck. I interviewed support and product teams, mapped the handoff, and replaced a manual approval step with a documented self-service flow. The revised process cut the average onboarding time from twelve days to seven." That paragraph shows analysis, cross-functional work, execution, and a result. It does not need to list every tool used.

Choose examples that are recent and relevant, but do not discard older work when it proves the exact capability the role requires. A career change letter may use an earlier project to show transferable skill. Be honest about the transition. Explain what carries over and what you are learning, rather than claiming identical experience where none exists.

The University of Washington advises applicants to use body paragraphs for stories or examples of skills in action, including the impact or outcome and its connection to the role [UW Career Center](https://careers.uw.edu/resources/102733/). That approach prevents a common error: turning the letter into a prose version of the resume. The resume contains the inventory. The letter explains the meaning of a selected item.

### 4. Show Your Enthusiasm and Cultural Fit

Enthusiasm is most credible when it is specific. Research the company beyond its slogan. Read the job description again, product documentation, public writing, recent releases, leadership interviews, open-source repositories, or customer material that relates to the role. Look for evidence about the work itself: the user problem, the stage of the product, the operating model, the technical challenge, or the community the company serves.

Then state what attracts you in plain language. "I want to join a mission-driven company" could fit almost any employer. "I want to work on developer education for a protocol where the public documentation and contributor path are part of the product" gives the reader something concrete. If you are applying to a Web3 organization, demonstrate that you understand the user responsibilities involved. For example, Ethereum's security guidance explains that users must not share recovery phrases or private keys and that transactions cannot usually be reversed [ethereum.org](https://ethereum.org/en/security/). A candidate for product, support, content, or community work can connect that reality to the need for exact instructions and responsible communication.

"Cultural fit" should not mean pretending to share every personal interest of the team. It means showing how you work in ways relevant to the role. You might value written decision records because you have worked across time zones. You might enjoy direct feedback because it improved your last project. You might care about user research because it changed a product decision you made. Give an example. Employers can assess a working style; they cannot assess an adjective such as "collaborative" without evidence.

Avoid flattery that you cannot support. Do not say a company is "the leader" or "revolutionary" unless you are quoting a verifiable source and the claim is relevant to your argument. Respectful, well-researched interest is enough. The letter is an application, not a press release.

### 5. End with a Clear Call to Action

The closing paragraph should be short. Reconnect your strongest qualification to the role, express interest in discussing the work, thank the reader, and provide contact details if they are not already in the application system. Do not demand an interview or promise results you cannot guarantee.

A direct closing could read: "My experience building content systems for technical audiences and my work with cross-functional launch teams would allow me to contribute quickly to this role. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss the position and the priorities for the first six months. Thank you for your time and consideration."

Use a standard sign-off such as "Sincerely" or "Best regards," followed by your name. If you attach a resume, portfolio, or writing sample, make sure the files have professional names and the links open without requiring the reader to request access. Proofread the closing with the same care as the opening. A company name from another application is an avoidable error.

### Cover Letter Structure

A useful structure is three or four paragraphs on one page. The UW Career Center recommends a concise format of no more than a half to one full page, with two body paragraphs as a common approach [UW Career Center](https://careers.uw.edu/resources/102733/). The page limit is a guide to editing, not an invitation to reduce the font until the letter becomes difficult to read.

| Paragraph | Content description |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | State the role, show your immediate connection to it, and give the reader a reason to continue. |
| 2 | Use one focused example to show a capability that the job requires. |
| 3 | Add a second example or explain why the company and work are a specific fit. |
| 4 | Reiterate interest, invite a conversation, and thank the reader. |

Before sending, read the letter once against the job description and once as if you were a busy hiring manager. On the first pass, check that every claim has evidence. On the second, remove sentences that repeat the resume, company boilerplate, or an idea already made. Read it aloud. Awkward phrases, repeated words, and claims that do not sound like you are easier to catch by ear.

### FAQs

#### What is the ideal length for a cover letter?

Keep it concise. Three or four short paragraphs generally fit within one page and give enough room for evidence. If the letter runs long, cut background first, then choose fewer examples. Do not remove the result from an example merely to save space. The result is often the line that proves the point.

#### Should I write a cover letter if it is optional?

Write one when you can make it specific and useful. An optional letter can explain a career change, connect a portfolio to the role, clarify a referral, or show evidence that is difficult to express in a resume. Do not submit a generic letter only to fill an upload field. A short, tailored note is stronger than a page that could accompany any application.

#### What tone should my cover letter have?

Use a professional, direct tone that sounds like a careful version of your normal writing. Let personality appear through the examples you select and the reasons you give for applying. Avoid jokes, inflated praise, and overly formal language. Clear sentences, accurate claims, and respect for the reader's time create a better impression than ornate wording.
