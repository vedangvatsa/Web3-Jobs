The [Web3](/what-is-web3) developer ecosystem is dynamic and competitive, with talent continually shifting among blockchain platforms. The overall number of Web3 developers has increased, yet that aggregate growth does not mean every [blockchain](/what-is-a-blockchain) ecosystem retains builders equally well. Developers move away from some chains and toward others. The movement is worth examining because it can reveal what makes a platform sustainable and attractive over time.

No single factor settles that decision. Developer experience, network effects, economic incentives, and shifting narratives overlap. A frustrating toolchain makes a small ecosystem harder to tolerate. A thin application layer makes the cost of learning a new system harder to justify. A short-lived funding model can make an otherwise promising environment feel unstable. A competing technical narrative can make a different path look more relevant. Looking at those factors together is more useful than treating migration as a simple popularity contest.

## Developer Experience Is an Operating Condition

Developer experience, often shortened to DX, is the first pressure point. If a platform is frustrating or poorly supported, developers have a reason to look elsewhere. That statement can sound obvious, but its implications are broad. A developer does not experience a chain only through its core technical design. They experience it through the ordinary tasks required to turn an idea into working software: setting up a project, compiling, testing, reading documentation, debugging, and finding a path through unfamiliar behavior.

### Tooling sets the pace of work

Inefficient compilers, unreliable testnets, and the absence of development frameworks hinder productivity. Each problem adds work that is separate from the application or protocol a developer intended to build. A toolchain need not be perfect to be useful, but it must allow developers to make progress without repeatedly reconstructing basic workflows.

The Ethereum Virtual Machine, or EVM, benefits from tools such as Foundry, which simplify the development lifecycle. The comparison matters because tools do more than save time on an isolated task. They can make practices repeatable across a community. When developers can use familiar tooling, share examples, and recognize a workflow, the cost of beginning or maintaining a project is lower. Platforms that lack comparable resources suffer because the cost of routine work rises before a developer has reached the problem that distinguishes the platform.

### Documentation is part of the product

Detailed, current documentation is non-negotiable. If developers struggle to use a platform's features because the documentation is poor, they are less likely to stay. Documentation is sometimes treated as a secondary communications task. In practice, it is the record that allows a developer to verify what a system expects, compare an example with actual behavior, and proceed without depending on private access to a small group of maintainers.

The issue is not merely the quantity of pages. A long document can still leave a builder uncertain about how to begin, test, or diagnose a failure. Conversely, a focused explanation can be valuable if it tells a developer what is supported, what assumptions apply, and where a workflow changes. The article's emphasis on current documentation is important here. A guide that does not match the system creates a hidden learning cost: the developer must determine whether a problem is theirs, the document's, or the platform's.

### Learning costs shape the talent pool

Platforms that rely on obscure programming languages without adequate educational resources face a separate challenge. Non-EVM chains often struggle to attract talent from the larger pool of EVM-native developers because they require a significant investment of time to learn. The point is not that a new language is inherently a flaw. A different language or model can be central to what a platform is trying to do. The question is whether the ecosystem provides a credible route for builders to acquire the needed knowledge.

## Network Effects Make Work More or Less Useful

Web3 is built around the combination of open-source protocols. Developers gravitate toward ecosystems rich in existing protocols because a new project does not begin from zero. It can connect to work that is already usable, understood, and supported by other participants. This is the composability argument: the value of an ecosystem is not limited to the chain itself, but includes the protocols, users, liquidity, applications, and shared expectations around it.

### The "empty city" problem

A new Layer 1 blockchain can resemble an empty city: there may be no users, liquidity, or existing decentralized applications, or dApps. Development in that setting can feel unproductive. The comparison captures a practical challenge rather than a judgment about the quality of a chain's technology. A builder needs other participants and components to test an idea in a meaningful environment. Without them, even a functioning application may lack the connections that make it useful.

### The gravity of the EVM

The [Ethereum](/what-is-ethereum) ecosystem has a strong network effect. It has a large user base, significant liquidity, stablecoins, and proven [DeFi](/what-is-defi) primitives. Launching a new dApp in that environment provides immediate access to existing infrastructure, making it a safer choice than launching on a non-EVM chain. The article points to the difficulty non-EVM chains face in gaining traction against the [Ethereum Layer 2 ecosystem](/guide-to-layer-2s) as an illustration of that pull.

"Safer" in this context is a relative development choice, not a guarantee about a project's outcome. Existing infrastructure can lower the number of foundational pieces a team must create before it can test its own contribution. A builder can concentrate more directly on the project at hand when the surrounding environment offers recognized primitives and connections. That is why composability is a retention issue as well as a launch issue. Once a developer has learned where the useful components are and how they connect, moving away carries a real cost.

## Incentives Must Survive Their First Cycle

Tokenomics and funding strategies significantly influence developer retention. The critical distinction is between a temporary reason to arrive and a durable reason to keep building. Incentives can help an ecosystem attract attention, fund work, or lower the immediate cost of participation. They become a retention problem when the ecosystem has not built a stable basis for work by the time the incentive changes.

### Short-term rewards can create a later exit

Ecosystems that rely on short-term, inflationary [token](/what-is-a-token) rewards often experience mass departures once those incentives diminish. Developers seek platforms with stable and predictable economic models. The article's concern is not with tokens in the abstract. It is with dependence on a reward structure that cannot support the same level of participation once its initial terms change.

For a builder, predictability affects planning. Work on a protocol, application, or tool may require more time than a short reward period provides. If the environment changes abruptly, a developer must decide whether the work can continue under the new conditions. That decision may be particularly difficult when the ecosystem has not supplied other reasons to remain, such as dependable tooling, active users, or public infrastructure.

### Public goods support the entire builder base

Healthy ecosystems invest in public goods, including core infrastructure and educational resources that benefit all builders. When projects fail to support those shared resources, developer engagement risks stagnation. This point links funding directly to DX and network effects. A test environment, a reference implementation, a documentation effort, or an educational resource may not belong to one application, yet it can make many applications easier to build and maintain.

## Narratives Change Where Builders Look

Narratives influence developer interest because they frame which technical problems appear urgent or promising. The earlier narrative centered on intense competition among Layer 1 blockchains, often described as the L1 wars. The focus has shifted toward a modular blockchain approach that emphasizes [Layer 2s](/guide-to-layer-2s) built on Ethereum. According to the article, this shift has led to substantial migration of developer talent to the Layer 2 ecosystem.

The contrast is useful because it shows that migration is not always a rejection of a chain's existing technology. Developers may move because a different organization of the stack appears to offer more room for the work they want to do. The modular framing changes where builders look for problems to solve and where teams organize their efforts. That can strengthen the existing pull of the Ethereum environment, since it adds new technical paths without requiring builders to leave the broader ecosystem.

The recent AI boom has also attracted many developers away from Web3 by offering challenges and opportunities that compete for top talent. This is a reminder that the relevant comparison is not always one chain against another. Web3 competes with other areas of technical work for attention. A platform that depends on scarce specialized talent must make a continuing case for why its tools, community, incentives, and technical direction justify the time required to build there.

## Two Migration Signals

The article identifies two cases that illustrate different aspects of the pattern. They should not be treated as interchangeable evidence. Each points to a distinct pressure on a developer community.

### Litecoin

The analysis of the [Litecoin developer slowdown](/litecoin-developer-activity-slowdown) highlights Litecoin's limited [smart contract](/what-are-smart-contracts) capabilities. That limitation produces fewer development opportunities and prompts developers to move to more flexible platforms. The relevance of this case is direct: if the range of work available on a chain is narrow, the ecosystem gives builders fewer reasons to stay or to begin new projects there.

### Solana

The decline in Solana's developer count was exacerbated by the collapse of its major supporter FTX, while the EVM-compatible Layer 2 ecosystem exerted a powerful draw. These pressures reflect how ecosystem conditions can interact: an external disruption can weaken confidence at the same time that an alternative offers a familiar technical and networked path. Despite those challenges, Solana's community remains resilient.

That final qualification matters. Migration and decline do not prove that a community has disappeared or that a chain has no future. They show why an ecosystem's ability to retain and support builders deserves ongoing attention. The broader lesson is methodological: assess developer movement alongside tooling, documentation, learning costs, composability, incentives, public goods, and competing narratives. A durable ecosystem is not defined by one headline. It is defined by whether builders can find useful, supported, and sustainable work after the headline has passed.
