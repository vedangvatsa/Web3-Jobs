Crypto journalism is not a job for someone who wants to repeat announcements more quickly than everyone else. It is reporting on money, software, companies, public networks, politics, and people whose incentives may be hard to see. The beat rewards curiosity about technical detail, but the core skill is still journalism: find out what happened, verify it, describe what is known, and make clear what remains unproved.

A protocol's token price is not a product review. A founder's social post is not confirmation. A transaction on a public ledger is evidence of a transfer between addresses, not evidence of the people or motives behind it. A good crypto journalist learns to make those distinctions without draining a story of its human stakes.

The job can include breaking news, investigations, explainers, policy coverage, markets, profiles, newsletters, podcasts, and research. You do not have to cover all of it. A better first move is to choose a subject you can follow deeply enough to develop judgment: decentralized finance, infrastructure, stablecoins, exchanges, developer tools, gaming, regulation, security incidents, or a region's local ecosystem.

## Learn the machinery well enough to ask better questions

You do not need to write production smart contracts to report on them. You do need to understand enough to recognize when an explanation is missing a key condition. Start with the concepts that recur in ordinary reporting: public and private keys, wallets, addresses, transactions, blocks, consensus, tokens, smart contracts, bridges, stablecoins, custody, and governance.

Learn the difference between a blockchain and a company built around it. Learn the difference between owning a token and holding equity. Learn the difference between a protocol interface, the smart contracts it calls, the entity that built it, and the people who control its domains or administration keys. Those distinctions often determine who can answer a question and what a claim actually means.

Use primary material before commentary. Read a project's documentation, governance proposal, technical specification, audit report, terms of service, court filing, or regulator notice. Read the whole item that supports the central claim, not a screenshot of one paragraph. If a project says it is decentralized, ask what that means in practice: who can change the contracts, control the front end, hold treasury assets, or block a user.

Build a personal glossary, but do not turn it into a substitute for reporting. Terms change meaning across projects. "TVL" might be a useful metric in a story, but readers need to know what assets are included and what it does not measure. "Community-owned" may describe a governance aspiration rather than a legal ownership right. Clear writing starts with refusing to let labels carry more evidence than they deserve.

## Use on-chain data as a reporting tool

Public blockchains give reporters material that many other beats do not: a durable record of transactions and smart-contract activity. A block explorer is a search interface for that record. Etherscan says an explorer can display transaction status, addresses, values, blocks, token transfers, and verified smart-contract code where available [in its explanation of the tool](https://info.etherscan.com/what-is-a-block-explorer/).

Learn to look up a transaction hash, inspect the addresses involved, check the token-transfer tab, and find the contract a wallet interacted with. In a straightforward story, that may verify that a transfer occurred at a stated time. In a more complex story, it may show that the transaction called a particular contract function or that several addresses moved funds in a pattern worth asking about.

The limitations are as useful as the capability. An address is not a legal name. It may belong to an exchange, a multisignature wallet, a bot, an individual, or a service acting for many customers. Labels on an explorer can be helpful leads, but they are not enough to prove ownership. A transfer does not establish a sale, a payment, a hack, a donation, or intent without additional evidence.

On-chain analytics platforms can help reporters aggregate data that would be impractical to read one transaction at a time. Dune provides a SQL editor and dashboards for querying and visualizing indexed blockchain data [in its documentation](https://docs.dune.com/home). Learn to inspect a query before citing its chart. Which chain does it cover? Which contracts are included? Is the time zone clear? Are values in native tokens or dollar estimates? Could a single contract or address change the apparent trend?

Save the query, the date you ran it, and the assumptions behind it. Data that is visible today may be re-indexed, relabeled, or interpreted differently later. A reader should be able to understand the method behind a claim such as "fees rose" or "active wallets declined." If the calculation is too complex to explain in a sentence, link the query or quote a named analyst who can explain it on the record.

## Verify before publishing

Crypto moves quickly, and that creates pressure to publish before facts settle. Do not let a fast social feed set your reporting standard. Reuters' journalistic standards say accuracy takes precedence over speed, encourage reporters to cross-check information, and advise them to be explicit about what they do not know [in their sourcing guidance](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/). Those are sound habits for every beat and particularly useful when a rumor can move a market.

Build a verification ladder. At the bottom are unverified posts, screenshots, anonymous messages, and claims from people who benefit if a story spreads. In the middle are a company's public statement, a blockchain transaction, a product change you can observe, or a document whose origin you have checked. Stronger evidence can include a court filing, regulator record, audited financial statement, source with direct knowledge who can explain their role, or multiple independent confirmations.

The ladder is not a formula that turns a source into truth. A company statement confirms what the company says; it may not confirm the substance of a disputed claim. An on-chain record confirms that the network recorded an event; it may not identify the actor. A named source can be wrong, self-interested, or repeating another person's account. The point is to state the evidence accurately and seek the strongest available support.

Call or message people through contact methods you independently verify. An impostor can copy a profile photo, use a nearly identical handle, or appear in a cloned group. For a high-stakes claim, ask a source to confirm through a known company email, an established phone number, or an introduction from someone you trust. Record the precise terms of an interview: on the record, background, or off the record. Do not change those terms later because the quote became useful.

When a source uses a pseudonym, confirm what you can about their role and access. A pseudonym is not evidence of dishonesty; it is common in crypto. It does change the reporting task. Ask how they know the information, look for documents or technical evidence, seek another source, and describe their anonymity without implying more certainty than you have.

## Report claims, not promotional language

Crypto projects are skilled at making ordinary developments sound conclusive. "Partnership" can mean a signed commercial agreement, a future integration, a joint event, or one company adding a logo to a webpage. "Mainnet launch" can mean code deployed with no users, a public product with restrictions, or a token event attached to a release. "Audit" can mean a limited review of a particular code version, not a guarantee that a system is safe.

Translate each label into a checkable fact. Instead of writing that a platform "secured a major partnership," say what the two parties announced, what they said they will do, and what is already live. Instead of writing that a project is "backed by" a fund, say whether the fund made an investment, received tokens, advises the project, or merely participated in a program.

Ask questions that force specifics. What contract address is live? Which users can access the product? What fees apply? Who controls upgrades? Was the amount raised cash, tokens, commitments, or a mixture? Is the claim based on a completed event or a plan? Can you provide the agreement, filing, dashboard, or named person who can verify it?

Use attribution with care. "The company said" tells readers that the statement came from the company. "According to a court filing" identifies a different kind of evidence. "The transaction data shows" is appropriate only when the data supports the exact statement. Do not write "reports say" or "the community believes." Find the report, the people, and the basis for the claim.

## Keep editorial independence visible

The beat has unusually direct conflicts. A journalist may hold the token being covered, receive airdrops, attend sponsored events, get free access to products, or be paid for consulting. These interests can affect coverage or appear to affect coverage even when the reporting is fair.

Create a personal conflicts policy before an assignment creates pressure. Disclose relevant holdings to your editor. Do not trade on material, nonpublic information learned through reporting. Do not accept tokens or fees in exchange for coverage. Do not let a source review a full article before publication. Reuters says its journalists should disclose actual or potential conflicts, avoid reporting on entities in which they or close family have an interest, and maintain a clear line between editorial and commercial work [in its independence standards](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/).

Freelancers need the same discipline in writing. If a protocol pays you to write its documentation, do not pitch an ostensibly independent investigation of that protocol without disclosing the relationship to the editor. If you make an opinionated investment newsletter, do not present it as straight reporting. Different forms can coexist, but readers and clients need to know which one they are reading.

Do not accept a source's framing that criticism is hostile and access is a reward for favorable coverage. A respectful reporter lets a subject respond to specific facts and publishes relevant answers. That is different from granting a subject control over the final story.

## Build a portfolio that shows reporting judgment

A portfolio should prove you can work a story, not merely explain terminology. Four strong samples can say more than twenty short summaries of press releases.

Write one reported news story. Choose a discrete event with documents and people you can contact. Explain what changed, who said so, what evidence supports the claim, and what is unresolved. Keep the lead factual. Do not turn a minor product update into a prediction about an entire market.

Write one technical explainer. Pick a narrow question, such as how a particular bridge transfers assets or what users authorize in a wallet signature. Read the primary documentation, interview a developer or security researcher, and explain the mechanism in plain language. The goal is not to impress a technical reader with vocabulary. It is to make a careful reader understand the mechanism and its limits.

Write one data-driven piece. Use an explorer or an analytics query to answer a modest question. Show the method, link the relevant data when possible, and ask an independent person to challenge your interpretation. Do not write "on-chain evidence proves" when the data only suggests a pattern.

Write one accountability story or policy analysis. This could examine a governance vote, a terms-of-service change, a legal filing, a security disclosure, or the gap between a public claim and an observable product. Give the subject a fair opportunity to respond. Strong work is precise about evidence and fair about uncertainty; it does not need theatrical language.

Publish under your name on a site you control or a platform with a stable public URL. Keep an index with date, format, and a one-line description of your role. If a piece involved an editor, designer, or data collaborator, say so. Editors hiring a reporter want to see what you actually did.

## Learn the newsroom side of the work

Editors hire people who can produce dependable copy under constraint. Practice filing a clean headline, a factual first paragraph, a short context paragraph, and a clear nut graph that tells the reader why the development affects them. Learn to write a correction. Learn to distinguish a confirmed fact from an allegation, estimate, forecast, or opinion.

Keep a source notebook. Record whom you spoke with, their role, contact details, reliability notes, relevant expertise, and any conflicts they disclosed. Protect sensitive reporting notes according to your outlet's security practices. Do not treat a directory of founders as a source network. The useful relationship is one in which people know you will quote them accurately, ask hard questions, and not expose private information carelessly.

Pitch with evidence of fit. Read an outlet's recent coverage, identify the editor who handles the subject, and send a short note that states the story, why it is timely, what reporting you can do, and why the outlet's readers would care. A pitch is not a full draft. It is an argument that you have a reportable story and can deliver it responsibly.

Jobs may be staff reporting roles, editorial fellowships, freelance assignments, newsletters, research desks, or communications roles. Do not confuse the last category with journalism. Communications work can be honorable and demanding, but its obligation is to a client or employer. Reporting's obligation is to the reader and the facts. Decide which work you are applying for, then label your portfolio accordingly.

Crypto needs reporters who can read code, documents, and data without losing sight of people affected by the results. The career is built article by article: accurate sourcing, visible methods, fair treatment of subjects, and a willingness to write "we do not yet know" when that is the honest answer.
