*This article explains general U.S. federal tax rules. It is not tax, legal, or financial advice. State, local, and non-U.S. rules can differ. Complex activity deserves a qualified tax professional who can review your complete records.*

Crypto tax work gets difficult when a wallet history is treated like a bank statement. It is not. A bank transfer usually moves dollars. A crypto transaction can sell one asset, receive another asset, pay a fee in a third asset, or create income at the same time. The public chain may show the movement, but it does not explain why it happened or which tax lot you used. Your records must supply that missing context.

For U.S. federal income tax purposes, the IRS treats digital assets as property, not currency. Its definition includes cryptocurrencies, stablecoins, and NFTs recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger or similar technology. That is the starting point for nearly every personal crypto question: did you acquire property, receive property as income, give property away, or dispose of property? The IRS sets out that approach in its [digital-assets guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) and its [virtual-currency FAQs](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions).

## Start with the transaction, not the app

Do not sort activity into "exchange," "wallet," or "DeFi" folders and assume the tax result follows the product name. Start with the legal and economic result of each entry. You need to know what you owned immediately before the transaction, what you received, what you gave up, and whether you received an asset for work, a reward, or an ownership interest.

Buying 0.2 ETH for dollars normally creates a new holding. The purchase establishes a cost basis and a holding period. It does not, by itself, create a capital gain or loss. Holding that ETH while its market price changes also does not create a realized gain or loss. The IRS says a taxpayer who only bought digital assets with real currency during the year may answer "No" to the digital-assets question on the return, provided no other listed transaction occurred. [The IRS instructions](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) also say a transfer between wallets or accounts you own or control is not itself a disposition, unless you paid the transfer fee with digital assets.

Selling ETH for dollars is a disposition. So is trading ETH for USDC, buying a laptop with BTC, or using crypto to pay a contractor. The fact that dollars never touch your bank account does not erase the first side of the transaction. The IRS states that an exchange of virtual currency held as a capital asset for another virtual currency, goods, or services produces capital gain or loss. See [FAQs 14 through 18](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions) for the agency's examples.

Stablecoins cause a common mistake here. Calling an asset "stable" describes its target price, not its tax category. A swap from ETH to a dollar-pegged token is still an exchange of one property interest for another. Record its dollar value at the time of the swap and calculate the result on the ETH disposed of. If your cost basis and the value received happen to be close, the gain or loss may be small. Small is not the same as absent.

Receiving crypto for services has a separate first step. If an employer pays wages in crypto, the fair market value when received is wages, subject to the usual federal withholding and payroll-tax rules. If an independent contractor receives crypto, its dollar value when received is ordinary income and may also be self-employment income. The same received value generally becomes the recipient's basis in the asset. The IRS addresses payment for services in [FAQs 9 through 13](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions). A later sale of those units is a new event. It can create capital gain or loss measured from that basis, even though the original receipt was already income.

## Cost basis is the working record

For a capital asset, gain or loss is usually:

`amount realized - adjusted basis = capital gain or capital loss`

The amount realized is usually the dollar value of what you received. Basis is generally what you paid to acquire the unit, including qualifying acquisition costs. The IRS describes basis as the amount of your investment in the property and notes that the basis of a digital asset includes acquisition costs such as fees and commissions in its [Form 8949 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8949).

Suppose you bought 1 ETH for $2,000 and paid a $20 acquisition fee. Your starting basis is $2,020. Months later, you exchange that ETH for tokens worth $2,700 after accounting for the exchange's stated value. Your gain before any needed basis adjustments is $680. The token you received starts with a basis tied to its value at that exchange. Keep both sides in your ledger. If you only record the new token, you will lose the evidence for the ETH disposition.

Holding period determines whether a capital result is short term or long term. The IRS says property held for one year or less is short term; property held more than one year is long term. Counting begins the day after acquisition and includes the day of disposition. Tax rates depend on the taxpayer's entire situation. Do not assume that every long-term result receives the same rate or that a loss will offset any form of income. [Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-544) and a preparer can help with the applicable rules.

Multiple purchases of the same coin create separate lots. You might buy BTC on three dates at three prices, then sell only part of the combined balance. The IRS permits specific identification when you can identify and substantiate the particular units. Its FAQ says acceptable records include the acquisition date and time, basis and fair market value, disposition date and time, and the value received for each unit. If you cannot specifically identify the units, the IRS says the earliest acquired units are treated as disposed of first, or FIFO. Read [FAQs 39 through 41](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions) before selecting a method in software.

Specific identification is a records question, not a button a platform can press retroactively. Preserve trade confirmations, wallet addresses, transaction hashes, exported CSV files, and a dated lot ledger. A tax app can organize this material. It cannot prove an unsupported assumption about a bridge, a wrapped token, an internal transfer, or a missing wallet.

## Income, rewards, and protocol activity

Income and capital gains are different categories. That distinction matters because a person can owe income tax when receiving an asset and later have a capital gain or loss when selling it. The IRS currently tells taxpayers to answer "Yes" to the digital-assets question if they received digital assets as a reward, from mining, staking, or similar activities. Its digital-assets page directs ordinary income from forks, staking, and mining to Schedule 1 where applicable. Review the [current IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets), because the agency's newer FAQ material applies different reporting rules to transactions from 2025 onward.

Do not force every protocol interaction into a short label. A liquidity position can involve contributing two assets, receiving a receipt token, collecting fees, and withdrawing a different mix later. A lending position can involve a deposit, a receipt token, reward distributions, and liquidation risk. A bridge can burn, lock, mint, or issue a representation depending on its design. The chain history is evidence, but the protocol terms and the actual assets received determine what happened.

For each unusual interaction, make a one-page transaction note while the facts are fresh. Include the protocol name, the date and time, wallet addresses, transaction hash, assets in, assets out, fees, a link to the applicable protocol documentation, and your description of purpose. Mark uncertain entries rather than inventing a classification. Bring those entries to a preparer early, not during the last week before filing.

NFT transactions deserve the same discipline. Buying an NFT establishes a basis. Selling it or paying with it can be a disposition. Creating an NFT, receiving royalties, or earning tokens for creative or technical work may raise income questions before any later capital transaction. The IRS includes NFTs within the broader digital-asset definition, but particular facts can also affect whether collectible rules or business-income rules apply. The agency has issued [separate NFT guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) and this is an area where individualized advice is sensible.

Hard forks and airdrops should not be merged into one word. Under the IRS FAQs, a hard fork without receipt of a new cryptocurrency does not create income. When a hard fork is followed by an airdrop and you receive units over which you have dominion and control, the IRS treats their fair market value when received as ordinary income. See [FAQs 22 through 25](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions). Keep the timestamp and the valuation source used. An explorer or exchange price is useful only if it represents a reasonable value for the asset at that moment.

## Fees, gifts, donations, and lost access

Network and trading fees need their own column. A fee paid in dollars is straightforward to record as a dollar cost. A fee paid with crypto may itself use property with a basis and a market value. The IRS expressly includes paying a transfer fee with digital assets among the events that require a "Yes" answer to the return question. Do not assume the fee is too minor to record. A complete ledger lets a professional determine the proper treatment instead of trying to rebuild small transactions from explorer data later.

Gifts are not sales between the giver and recipient, but they require records. The recipient's basis and holding period can depend on the donor's basis and documentation. If an asset is genuinely given, retain the date, units, donor information where appropriate, and any records establishing the donor's basis. The IRS explains the different gain and loss basis rules for gifts in [FAQs 31 through 33](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions).

Donating crypto to a qualified charitable organization can avoid recognizing gain or loss on the donation, according to [IRS FAQ 34](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/frequently-asked-questions-on-virtual-currency-transactions). That does not mean every claimed deduction is automatic. The holding period, valuation, substantiation, and appraisal rules can matter. Obtain the organization's acknowledgement and ask a tax professional about Form 8283 and appraisal requirements before completing a large donation.

Lost keys, a frozen platform account, an exploit, a collapsed token, and a price decline do not share one automatic tax result. Preserve all evidence of the event and do not report a loss merely because the market price is lower. A tax professional must apply the current rules to the actual facts. This is one reason to retain account statements and wallet records even for assets that appear worthless.

## Filing with defensible records

Begin your filing work before tax season. Build one ledger with every exchange account, self-custody wallet, chain, payroll source, and protocol. Reconcile transfers between your own addresses so they are not counted as sales. Then reconcile each imported trade against the platform statement and against the wallet transaction where relevant. Missing cost basis is a research task, not a reason to enter zero without support.

The IRS says capital-asset dispositions are generally reported on Form 8949 and summarized on Schedule D. For tax year 2025, the Form 8949 instructions added digital-asset boxes and explain when a Form 1099-DA may report proceeds or basis. [Read the instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8949) for the year you are filing, not a blog post about an earlier form. A broker statement can help reconcile data, but it may not see your prior wallet purchases, your transfers, or every fee.

Keep the following package with your return work: exports from every platform; a transaction ledger; valuation method notes; lot-identification records; copies of Forms 1099-DA, W-2, or 1099 statements; protocol notes for unusual activity; and a PDF or spreadsheet that explains manual adjustments. Store it securely. A future preparer should be able to trace a reported number back to the underlying transaction without needing your memory.

If your activity is a handful of purchases and one sale, this process can be brief. If it includes compensation, frequent swaps, NFTs, bridges, staking, or activity across chains, treat records as part of operating your portfolio. The useful goal is not a perfect-looking dashboard. It is a return that matches a clear, documented account of what you acquired, received, and disposed of.
