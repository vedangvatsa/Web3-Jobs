There is no single "Web3 salary." A smart-contract auditor, protocol engineer, product designer, community lead, legal operations hire, and growth contractor do not share one labor market or one compensation structure. Companies also use the label Web3 for very different businesses: custodians, wallets, exchanges, developer tools, gaming studios, infrastructure providers, token networks, and ordinary software companies that happen to accept crypto.

Treat the job offer as a set of cash flows, rights, restrictions, and risks. The headline total is only a starting point. An offer can look generous because it adds four years of a token grant to one year of salary, uses the last funding-round price for a private asset, or quotes a large token count without saying when you can sell. Another offer can look modest while providing a high cash base, reliable benefits, and a clear path to a larger review after the first year.

The practical question is not "Is this a good Web3 salary?" It is "What will I be paid, when will I control it, what can change, and what am I giving up to take the risk?" Write the answers down before you compare two offers.

## Separate cash from everything else

Cash salary is the most dependable part of an offer if it is stated in a familiar currency, paid on a fixed schedule, and backed by an employer that can meet payroll. Record the annual base, pay frequency, currency, location adjustment, and whether the role is employee or contractor. Do not blend a discretionary bonus, a one-time signing payment, or an estimated token value into base salary.

For context, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a May 2025 median annual wage of $135,980 for software developers and $120,230 for data scientists. Those are national medians for broad occupations, not Web3 pay bands and not evidence that a particular offer is fair. They are still useful anchors when a company tries to make an uncertain token package sound equivalent to cash. See the [software-developer data](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm) and [data-scientist data](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/math/data-scientists.htm) for the scope and limits of those figures.

If the role is remote, ask which jurisdiction governs pay, tax withholding, leave, overtime rules, and termination. "Work from anywhere" can mean a local employment relationship through an employer of record, a direct employment relationship in one country, or a contractor agreement. These arrangements affect cash predictability, benefits, intellectual-property terms, and the work required to handle taxes. They are not paperwork details to read after accepting.

List every cash item separately:

- Annual base salary and payment currency.
- Guaranteed bonus and the exact conditions for earning it.
- Signing bonus, repayment terms, and payment date.
- Annual review or promotion process.
- Health coverage, retirement contribution, paid leave, parental leave, equipment, and professional-development budget.
- Contractor rate, expected hours, invoice schedule, and notice period.

A contractor rate should not be compared directly with an employee salary. The contractor may need to fund insurance, retirement, equipment, taxes, paid time off, and gaps between engagements. A higher hourly number can still produce less predictable annual income. Ask for the expected monthly workload and whether the company can reduce it without paying a minimum.

## Equity and tokens are separate instruments

Company equity and network tokens are often mentioned together, but they are different rights. Equity may represent an ownership interest in a legal entity and may come through options, restricted stock, RSUs, or another plan. A token grant may be a contractual right to receive tokens later, a current transfer subject to restrictions, a grant from a foundation, or a reward program. The documents, tax treatment, governance rights, liquidity, and enforcement path can differ.

Do not accept the phrase "you get ownership" without asking what you actually receive. Request the plan or grant agreement, not only the recruiting slide. Identify the granting entity. Is it the operating company, a foundation, a subsidiary, or another party? Does the grant survive a merger, shutdown, role change, leave, or move to another country? Is it subject to board, foundation, or administrator discretion? Can the issuer change the program or the supply schedule?

For equity, ask for the number of shares or units, the type of award, exercise price if relevant, vesting schedule, cliff, expiration date, and any acceleration terms. Ask for the most recent company valuation only as context. A private-company valuation is not a cash price you can realize on demand. A share count is also difficult to interpret without a fully diluted share count and a clear explanation of dilution risk.

For tokens, ask for the number of tokens, the contract or asset identifier, the date the right is granted, vesting and release schedule, lockups, transfer restrictions, clawback rules, and the legal entity that promises delivery. Ask whether tokens exist today, whether a launch is only planned, and what event must occur before the grant becomes deliverable. "Expected token generation event" is not a payment date.

Do not put a token's entire quoted value in your personal budget. A grant's stated value is commonly calculated as token quantity multiplied by a reference price. That is arithmetic, not a guarantee of proceeds. Your eventual proceeds depend on how many tokens vest, whether they become transferable, market price when you can sell, trading depth, taxes, and any contractual or legal restrictions. If the tokens are illiquid or the market is thin, a displayed price may not represent the price available for your whole position.

Use three columns in your own comparison: cash you expect to receive, contingent compensation that may vest, and speculative upside. Only the first column should pay fixed bills. The second can be valuable, but it needs a calendar and documents. The third may motivate you to join. It should not be used to justify a cash package that is below your minimum.

## Read the vesting schedule as a calendar

Vesting is the schedule under which you earn a grant. A four-year grant with a one-year cliff means no scheduled vesting before the first anniversary, then a specified portion may vest, with the rest vesting monthly or quarterly afterward. The details control the outcome. A statement that an award "vests over four years" is incomplete until you know the cliff, cadence, start date, acceleration, and what happens when employment ends.

Build a calendar with actual dates. For each month or quarter, note the cash salary expected, equity that vests, tokens that vest, tokens that become transferable, and any window in which selling is allowed. Mark the largest events in the first two years. This exercise catches comparisons that a total-compensation number hides.

Distinguish vesting from liquidity. Tokens can vest while remaining locked. Equity can vest while remaining private. An option can vest while requiring cash to exercise. A sale restriction can last beyond vesting. An employee may also need company approval or face a trading policy. "Vested" means a contractual interest has been earned. It does not necessarily mean money is available.

Ask what happens in ordinary departure cases. If you resign, are terminated without cause, are laid off, or are moved to a contractor role, which unvested awards are forfeited? Do you have a short period to exercise options? Are tokens that have vested but remain restricted retained, cancelled, or delayed? Is there acceleration on a change of control? Get the answer in the plan text or an offer addendum. A verbal assurance is not a vesting term.

Some offers use milestone vesting rather than time vesting. That can be reasonable when a role has a defined launch or delivery target. It can also be risky if the milestone is vague or controlled by people who can redefine it. Replace "when the product ships" with a named feature, objective acceptance criteria, an approving party, and a date by which the milestone is deemed met or the grant outcome is resolved.

## Tax questions need an answer before payment

Tax treatment depends on your residency, employment status, the asset, the grant documents, and timing. Do not assume a token grant is tax-free because it has not been sold. In the United States, the IRS treats digital assets as property, and it says income from digital assets is taxable. Its [digital-assets page](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) states that digital assets paid as employee wages are reportable as wages, while digital assets paid to independent contractors are generally business income. It also states that later dispositions of capital assets can create capital gain or loss.

Ask the company how it will report and withhold. Will it issue a W-2, a 1099, a local payroll statement, or nothing because it treats you as an overseas contractor? Will it withhold cash to cover taxes on a crypto payment? If it pays only tokens, do you have enough cash to meet tax obligations at the relevant time? A good answer names the employer or payer, the country, the form, the valuation method, and the pay date. "Talk to your accountant" is reasonable advice, but it is not a substitute for the company's own reporting plan.

Maintain your own ledger of every crypto payment, grant, vesting event, restriction release, and sale. Note the date and time, unit count, dollar value used, wallet or platform, transaction hash when available, and tax document received. The IRS requires records sufficient to establish the position taken on a return and explains basis and disposition reporting in its [digital-asset guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets). This record is useful even if the company promises a dashboard or tax form.

For equity and token grants with unusual restrictions, pay for personal tax advice before signing rather than trying to repair the decision later. The cost is small compared with misunderstanding a taxable event, exercise deadline, or cross-border contractor obligation. The advisor needs the actual agreements, not a recruiter summary.

## Evaluate the company behind the package

Compensation promises are only useful if the issuer can honor them. For a cash package, ask which legal entity employs you, where payroll is processed, and whether the company has an established payroll provider. For a grant, ask who controls the cap table or token allocation and who has authority to approve exceptions. For a protocol organization, understand whether the operating company and the token or foundation entity are separate.

You do not need confidential financial statements to ask sensible questions. Ask what the role is intended to accomplish in the next twelve months, who owns the budget, who your manager is, and how the company decides that a team is succeeding. Ask whether the position is backfill, expansion, or tied to a particular funding event. Ask how many people on the immediate team have left recently and why the role is open. Specific answers are more informative than assurances about "runway" or community enthusiasm.

Review the work itself. High-risk compensation is easier to accept when you have genuine interest in the work, a manager who can make decisions, and skills that will remain valuable if the token never does. A role with clear engineering, research, product, security, or operations experience can improve your next option even if the current company changes course. A vague role with low cash and a large contingent grant may leave you with neither predictable income nor clear evidence of impact.

Security and reputation are part of the risk check. If the job touches wallets, customer assets, smart contracts, or signing authority, ask about code review, incident response, access controls, and who has authority during an emergency. You are evaluating the work environment as well as the token. A large theoretical grant does not compensate for a role that expects one person to carry uncontrolled operational risk.

## Compare offers on the same basis

Make one simple worksheet for every offer. Put cash base in the first row. Add only guaranteed cash payments due in the first year. List benefits separately instead of inventing a dollar figure unless you have a real cost estimate. Put equity and tokens in a separate section with their vesting dates, estimated values, restrictions, and your confidence level. Add a final section for employment status, working hours, notice period, location, and tax reporting.

Then run three scenarios. In the cash-only scenario, ignore equity and tokens entirely. Can you meet your commitments and still accept the role? In the ordinary scenario, use a cautious value for the portion likely to vest and become usable. In the downside scenario, assume the speculative award becomes worthless or inaccessible while you still need to handle any tax and career consequences. If the offer works only in the most optimistic scenario, it is not a stable compensation package.

Negotiate the part that reduces a real risk. You might ask for a higher cash base in exchange for fewer tokens, a signing bonus, a shorter cliff, more frequent vesting, a written review date, an extended option-exercise window, accelerated vesting on a layoff, clearer token-delivery terms, or a contractor minimum. Explain the request in terms of the issue. "I need cash certainty because the token is locked for twelve months" is easier to address than "I want more upside."

The final offer should let you explain the arrangement in a few direct sentences: who pays you, how much cash arrives each month, what other instrument you receive, when it vests, when it can be used or sold, what happens if you leave, and how the payer will report it. If those sentences cannot be written from the documents, the offer is not ready to evaluate. Ask for clarity before you accept the risk.
