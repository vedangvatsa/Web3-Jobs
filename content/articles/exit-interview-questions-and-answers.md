An exit interview is a structured conversation with a person who has already decided to leave. Its purpose is to learn about the work experience, not to reopen the resignation negotiation. That distinction affects the tone of every question. A departing employee should be able to describe a poor handoff, an unclear career path, a difficult manager, or a better offer without worrying that the meeting will turn into a debate.

The strongest exit process treats each account as evidence, not a verdict. One employee may have a personal conflict or a role mismatch. Several people describing the same approval bottleneck, manager behavior, pay practice, or on-call burden point to a condition worth examining. The [U.S. Office of Personnel Management's exit-survey guidance](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/data-analysis-documentation/employee-surveys/exit-survey-guide.pdf) makes a related point: organizations need a consistent instrument and a way to analyze results across groups if they want the information to support decisions.

For a Web3 company, the setting may include extra sources of friction: market-driven compensation volatility, remote teams across time zones, security-sensitive access, token incentives, or unclear ownership between a foundation, a protocol team, and a commercial company. Those details deserve direct questions. They should not be used to explain away ordinary management problems.

## Set the conditions for an honest conversation

Invite the employee after their resignation has been acknowledged and before their final days become entirely administrative. A voluntary, confidential interview conducted by a people-operations partner, HR professional, or trained third party usually produces more usable answers than one led by the direct manager. The manager still needs feedback, but the person whose behavior may be under discussion is a poor choice to collect it.

Explain what confidentiality means before the first question. Do not promise secrecy that the company cannot keep. A practical statement is: individual comments will be summarized, names will not be attached to routine reports, and allegations involving harassment, discrimination, fraud, threats, or safety may require investigation. In the United States, reports about protected-class discrimination or harassment can trigger duties under laws enforced by the [Equal Employment Opportunity Commission](https://www.eeoc.gov/overview), so a company should have a defined escalation path rather than improvising after the call.

Use a private video call or a quiet room, allow enough time, and tell the employee they may skip any question. Do not record by default. Recording can alter candor and can create consent and data-retention obligations. If a company needs a recording for accessibility or note accuracy, it should obtain clear permission, limit access, and apply its stated retention policy. Notes should distinguish what the employee said from the interviewer's interpretation.

Start with a simple frame: the organization wants to improve the job for current and future colleagues; it is not asking the employee to defend leaving; and it will not use the conversation to challenge their account. Ask for examples, dates, and effects where helpful, but do not cross-examine. The goal is enough detail to identify a fix, not enough detail to identify the speaker in a small team.

## Questions about the decision to leave

Open with: **What first caused you to consider another role?** This invites a timeline. Listen for a single precipitating event, such as a reorganization or a denied promotion, but also for a slow accumulation of concerns. Ask a neutral follow-up: **What had been happening before that point?** The difference matters. A counteroffer cannot repair a year of insufficient feedback, chronic weekend work, or an expectation that changed after hiring.

Ask: **What made the new opportunity the right next step?** The answer separates pull factors from push factors. A new role may offer work the current company cannot reasonably provide, such as a different specialty, location, or seniority. That is not necessarily a retention failure. If several people leave for clearer leveling, more predictable pay, stronger management, or better engineering practices, those answers are comparable and can inform a broader review.

Ask: **If one realistic thing had changed six months ago, would it have altered your decision?** "Realistic" is important. It steers the employee away from an abstract wish list and toward a condition the employer could have addressed. Examples might include a staffed project, a written compensation policy, a manager who held regular one-to-ones, or a clearer rule for token grants. Do not treat a "no" as a failed interview. It can mean the employee's move is a healthy career decision.

Ask: **Did the role you joined match the role you performed?** Compare the answer with the job description, offer letter, performance goals, and the work actually assigned. A gap may expose recruiting language that oversold autonomy, a post-hire reprioritization that was never acknowledged, or scope growth without title and pay review. In a protocol organization, this question can surface whether an employee was hired by one entity but expected to support another without clear authority.

## Questions about management and daily work

Ask: **What did your manager do that helped you succeed, and what should they have done differently?** This phrasing asks for both evidence and improvement rather than inviting a broad rating. Follow with questions about priority setting, feedback, decision rights, staffing, and access to the manager. "They were not supportive" is a starting point. "They changed priorities in weekly meetings without updating deadlines" is a condition a manager and leadership team can address.

Ask: **Did you have the tools, information, access, and training needed to do the job?** For technical teams, this may mean documentation, test environments, incident runbooks, code review capacity, hardware wallets, or production access. For compliance and operations teams, it may mean reliable transaction data, vendor contracts, case-management tools, and a defined escalation channel. Ask which missing resource caused the most repeated delay and what workaround the employee used. Workarounds are often where operational risk becomes visible.

Ask: **How were goals set, changed, and evaluated?** A useful answer describes the cadence, not only the outcome. Teams can tolerate a changing market or a protocol incident when leaders explain the reason, tradeoffs, and new owner. They struggle when priorities move silently and performance reviews later judge work against retired objectives. The [Society for Human Resource Management](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/tools/toolkits/managing-employee-performance) describes performance management as an ongoing process; an exit interview can test whether that process existed in practice.

Ask: **What parts of the work made it hard to sustain a healthy schedule?** Avoid assuming that every late night is a commitment problem. Ask about on-call rotation, meeting hours, travel, incident response, staffing, and unplanned work. A remote company should also ask whether colleagues in particular regions repeatedly carried inconvenient meeting windows. Aggregate this information by team and tenure; a single heroic employee can conceal an unsound operating model.

Ask: **Did you receive useful recognition and feedback?** Recognition is not a substitute for pay or advancement, but its absence can reveal a weak management routine. Ask for the kind of feedback they received, how often, and whether it arrived in time to be useful. Vague praise after a launch does little for someone who needed a direct discussion about quality or growth months earlier.

## Questions about pay, progression, and fairness

Ask: **How clear were the compensation, equity, and token-incentive arrangements?** Do not demand the new employer's offer. The useful question is whether the former employer's own terms were understandable, administered as promised, and reviewed at sensible intervals. In token-based organizations, employees may need clear information about vesting, lockups, tax treatment, governance rights, and what happens if their employment ends. Written terms and access to qualified legal or tax advice are more valuable than informal assurances.

Ask: **Could you see a credible next step in your career here?** Follow with: **What would that next step have required, and who made it clear?** A company may not be able to offer a promotion, but it can explain its levels, give specific development feedback, provide chances to demonstrate scope, and say when a decision will be revisited. Repeated reports of opaque promotion decisions warrant a calibration review, not a generic training session.

Ask: **Did you observe practices that felt inconsistent or unfair?** Give the employee room to discuss workload distribution, access to opportunities, pay, performance ratings, credit for work, or treatment by region or employment type. Do not ask them to make a legal conclusion. Ask what happened, who was involved, whether it was raised at the time, and what records may exist. Route allegations through the appropriate independent process; do not fold them into a quarterly trend chart.

## Questions about culture and organizational design

Ask: **How would you describe the way decisions were made here?** This gets closer to culture than asking for adjectives. An employee can describe whether decisions had named owners, whether dissent was safe, whether information traveled reliably, and whether leaders followed through. For decentralized projects, ask whether governance proposals, forum discussion, and off-chain team decisions were understandable to employees. "Decentralization" does not answer who can approve a budget or resolve a production incident.

Ask: **What should the company preserve?** Exit interviews are not only a fault-finding exercise. Specific positives identify practices worth protecting during growth: a useful onboarding buddy system, thoughtful code review, transparent founder updates, a customer-support handoff, or a manager who develops junior staff. Repeated positive examples can be turned into operating standards rather than left as compliments in a spreadsheet.

Ask: **What should change first?** Request one concrete change and ask what it would affect. Employees often identify a narrow intervention: publish role levels, consolidate status meetings, fund a security review before launch, or document who approves a compliance exception. Resist turning every answer into a company-wide initiative. The change should match the evidence and the team that owns it.

Close with: **Would you recommend this company to someone whose work you respect? Why or why not?** The explanation is more informative than the yes-or-no answer. Then ask: **What have we not asked that we should understand?** The final open question often surfaces an issue the interview script did not anticipate. Thank the employee, explain the next administrative steps, and do not pressure them to disclose confidential information from their next employer.

## Turn comments into accountable work

Code responses consistently after each interview. Useful categories might include manager practices, role design, compensation clarity, career progression, workload, tools, culture, and voluntary pull factors. Preserve the original notes separately from the coding decision. A person who says "I left for pay" may also describe unclear progression and a manager who never discussed a review; reducing the answer to one label loses the operational signal.

Review trends on a regular cadence with enough respondents to protect anonymity. Segment carefully by function, manager layer, location, tenure band, and employment type only where group size permits. Compare exit themes with retention data, engagement feedback, promotion rates, recruiting-decline reasons, incident records, and workload measures. Correlation is not proof, but it can identify where a focused review is warranted.

Assign an owner and a deadline to any action the organization accepts. "Improve culture" is not an action. "Publish the engineering on-call policy by June, then review overnight pages by region each quarter" can be checked. Leadership should also record why it declined a recommendation. That record prevents the same issue from being rediscovered in every interview without a decision ever being made.

Share the themes and completed actions with current employees without exposing individuals. A short update might say that several departures identified unclear promotion expectations, that published levels and promotion criteria are being introduced, and that the people team will report adoption after two cycles. Do not claim that every concern has been solved. Credibility comes from describing the evidence, the chosen response, and the limits of that response.

Exit interviews are one input, not a substitute for regular listening. A company that waits for resignations to discover that managers are unavailable or goals are unclear is collecting feedback too late. Regular one-to-ones, stay interviews, fair performance processes, and safe reporting channels give employees a chance to shape their work while they are still there. The exit interview then becomes what it should be: a respectful final conversation and a disciplined source of lessons.
