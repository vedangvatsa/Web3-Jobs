The first 90 days are an information-gathering period, a relationship-building period, and the start of a delivery record. They are not a test of whether you can solve every problem before you understand the business. The goal is to leave the period with a shared view of your responsibilities, a reliable way of working with your manager and peers, a few completed commitments, and an agreed next set of priorities.

The plan below is deliberately practical. It applies to an office, a remote team, a startup, a large company, a protocol team, or a DAO-funded role, but it should be adapted to the actual job. A new employee joining an incident-response team needs operational access and escalation training immediately. A researcher may need time to understand evidence standards. A manager inheriting a team must learn the work and establish trust without delaying necessary people decisions. Use the stages as prompts, not as a script.

## Before the first day

Ask your manager for the first week's schedule, hardware and account requirements, required reading, and the people you should meet. If the company has an onboarding portal, complete the required forms before the start date where possible. This reduces wasted time, but it does not replace a conversation about the role.

Prepare a short working profile for yourself. Include your preferred communication style, working hours, areas of prior expertise, what you are learning, and any constraints that affect meetings or travel. You do not need to disclose personal details to be useful. The point is to make it easier for colleagues to work with you and to establish a habit of stating constraints early.

Read public material with care. Study the company's product, customers where appropriate, recent releases, business model, and stated priorities. For a Web3 organization, read the protocol documentation, current governance process, security practices, and developer repository before treating community commentary as fact. Write down questions, but resist arriving with a diagnosis. Public information gives context; it rarely explains why an internal process exists or what trade-off the team has already made.

## Days 1 through 30: Build a usable map

The first month is for orientation and observation. Set up access, complete required training, and learn how work moves from request to decision to delivery. Missing access is not a trivial administrative issue if it prevents you from reviewing the systems or documents that define the job. Keep a list of blocked access and ask for help with a clear explanation of the work it prevents.

Have an early one-on-one with your manager. Ask what outcomes matter in the next quarter, what you own directly, what decisions require their approval, what work should be deprioritized, and how they prefer to receive updates. Ask how they will judge progress at 30, 60, and 90 days. A role description may be broad or outdated; this conversation turns it into an operating agreement.

Write down what you heard and send a concise recap. It can state: "My understanding is that I own X, will support Y, and will bring Z decisions to you. By the end of the quarter, success looks like A and B. Please correct anything I have missed." This is not bureaucratic. It gives your manager a low-effort chance to correct a mismatch before it becomes a performance issue.

Meet the people who supply your work, depend on your work, approve decisions, or hold important institutional knowledge. Ask each person what their team is trying to achieve, how your role intersects with it, what is currently difficult, and how they prefer to work. The question "What should a new person understand before changing this?" often produces better context than "What is broken?"

Make a stakeholder map, but keep it simple. Record a person's role, relationship to your work, current priorities, open questions, and promised follow-up. Do not reduce colleagues to political categories. The map is a memory aid for understanding dependencies and honoring commitments. It should not become a private dossier of gossip.

Learn the team's written and unwritten operating norms. Notice where decisions are recorded, how emergencies are escalated, what happens in planning meetings, who reviews work, and whether disagreement happens in documents, chat, or live discussion. The [GitLab handbook on asynchronous communication](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/asynchronous/) offers useful principles for distributed teams: give context in writing, make decisions discoverable, and avoid requiring everyone to be online at the same time. Apply the principles in a way that fits the team rather than importing a new process on day three.

Deliver one or two small, well-scoped commitments. Choose work that matters to the team and can be completed without pretending to know everything. A good early contribution might clarify a handoff, close a documented bug, prepare a meeting with a clear decision, improve an onboarding page, or finish an analysis that has been waiting for capacity. Confirm the definition of done before starting. Finishing a modest task reliably teaches colleagues more about you than joining every conversation.

Keep a learning log. Each entry can contain the question, the answer or source, the person who helped, and any related decision. This prevents repeated questions and exposes gaps in documentation. It also creates useful material for your first-month conversation with your manager. Do not use the log to hoard information; turn recurring answers into shared documentation when appropriate.

## Calibrate with the person who evaluates your work

Your manager may care about outcomes, judgment, communication, team health, technical depth, or some combination. Ask for examples of work they considered strong in the past and the failure modes they want to avoid in this role. A manager who says "be proactive" may mean bring options before a deadline; another may mean resolve routine issues without waiting for approval. Turning broad expectations into examples gives you something practical to test during the first month.

Agree on the shape of your one-on-one meetings. A weekly conversation can cover decisions, risks, feedback, and career questions, while a written update handles routine status. Bring an agenda rather than expecting the manager to reconstruct your priorities from chat. If you need a decision, state the decision, your recommendation, the alternatives, and the date at which delay changes the outcome. This makes it easier for a busy manager to respond with useful direction.

Do not use the first 30 days to make a silent promise that you will absorb unlimited work. If the job description, inherited backlog, and new requests point to more than one person can complete, make the capacity problem visible. Estimate only enough to compare options, then ask the accountable person to choose a sequence. Early clarity about capacity is more constructive than a late explanation that every request was treated as urgent.

Pay attention to how feedback reaches you. If your manager gives a correction in a meeting, clarify the behavior or output they want instead, then follow up with the revised approach. If feedback is vague, ask for a recent example and the effect it had. You do not need to agree with every interpretation immediately, but you do need to understand the expectation that will be used to assess your work. Keep your own record of feedback and the action you took so the 60- and 90-day conversations can be specific.

## Days 31 through 60: Turn knowledge into dependable work

By the second month, you should have enough context to take accountable ownership of a meaningful workstream. Pick one outcome with a visible user, stakeholder, or operational benefit. Define the problem, scope, dependencies, decision-maker, risks, and delivery check. Agree these with your manager before announcing a large initiative.

This is the right time to establish your working rhythm. Send status updates at a cadence your manager finds useful. A strong update is brief: completed work, next work, decisions or risks that need attention, and changes from the prior plan. Avoid reporting a long list of effort without an outcome. If something is blocked, name the blocker, owner, impact, and the help you need.

Ask for feedback while there is time to use it. Instead of a vague "How am I doing?", try focused questions: "Was the level of detail in that update useful?" "What part of this handoff created extra work for you?" "What would make you more confident in my ownership of this area?" Focused questions produce specific answers and signal that feedback can be acted on without drama.

Begin to form an independent view of the work, but show your evidence. If you see a possible improvement, explain the current process as you understand it, the cost it creates, the alternatives you considered, the trade-offs, and the smallest safe experiment. Do not describe an existing system as irrational merely because it is unfamiliar. Teams often carry constraints involving customers, regulation, legacy architecture, security, or hard-won lessons from a past incident.

For project work, make the work visible enough that others can help. The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html) describes transparency, inspection, and adaptation as the basis of its framework. Even if your team does not use Scrum, the underlying practice is sound: maintain a clear goal, inspect progress against it, and adjust when evidence changes. A board, a short project page, or a regular written update can serve that purpose if the team actually uses it.

Build relationships through useful collaboration, not forced networking. Follow through on a request from a peer. Give credit in a meeting. Share a relevant document before someone has to ask. Learn how a partner measures success. Trust grows when people experience you as prepared, candid, and dependable, especially when a commitment must change.

Protect focus as requests accumulate. You do not need to accept every task to be helpful. When new work arrives, clarify its urgency, expected result, deadline, and what should move if you take it on. A polite response can be: "I can start this today, but it will move the analysis we agreed to deliver Friday. Which is the priority?" This keeps prioritization with the person who can make the trade-off.

## Days 61 through 90: Agree on ownership and the next quarter

The final month should demonstrate that you can operate with context, not that onboarding has ended. Complete or materially advance the workstream you selected. Document what you delivered, the evidence of value, unresolved risks, and recommended next steps. If the result did not meet its original target, explain the change plainly. A trustworthy account of a partial result is preferable to a polished story that hides a missed assumption.

Schedule a 90-day conversation with your manager. Bring a short written summary: what you learned about the business and team, work completed, feedback received and acted on, current responsibilities, risks or capacity concerns, and proposed objectives for the next quarter. Ask whether the manager's view matches yours. This conversation should settle the difference between being helpful in many places and owning the right work.

Objectives should be specific enough to guide weekly choices. "Improve onboarding" is a direction, not an objective. "Reduce the time to provision a new support account by documenting the approval path and testing it with two new hires" gives a scope, outcome, and way to evaluate progress. Some objectives will remain qualitative, particularly in leadership, research, and relationship work. In that case, define observable behaviors and review points rather than inventing a false metric.

Discuss development in the context of team needs. You might need deeper product knowledge, a technical skill, negotiation practice, management coaching, or exposure to a partner team. Ask for a concrete opportunity: ownership of a small launch, participation in an incident review, a mentor meeting, or time to study a critical system. Career growth becomes more credible when it is connected to useful future work.

## Learn the controls before taking shortcuts

New hires often encounter friction while waiting for accounts, permissions, or approvals. Treat that friction as information. It may exist because a system holds customer data, production credentials, financial authority, or code that can affect users. Ask for the documented route, the approver, and the expected timing. Do not borrow another person's account, copy credentials into an unapproved tool, or ask a colleague to make an unreviewed production change on your behalf just to complete an early task.

For a technical or Web3 role, identify the systems that require separate handling: source control, cloud access, customer-support tools, signing devices, treasury operations, deployment pipelines, and incident channels. Learn who owns access reviews and how an urgent request is handled. NIST's [SP 800-53](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-53r5.pdf) includes account management among its access-control families, with authorization and review responsibilities. Your employer's procedure will be more specific, but the underlying reason is the same: access should match a defined role and be reviewable.

Before you automate or connect a new service, ask where data will go and who approves the integration. A personal spreadsheet, unapproved AI service, or convenience bot can create a data-handling problem even when the intent is harmless. If the team has approved tools and retention rules, learn them before importing documents or customer information. If no rule is apparent, raise the question with the relevant owner instead of setting a precedent alone.

The same caution applies to organizational knowledge. You may learn about compensation, performance issues, security findings, partner negotiations, or an unreleased launch. Share it only with people who need it for their work and use the team's designated channels. Building trust includes showing that colleagues can give you context without losing control of sensitive information.

At this point, you can also identify a process to improve, provided you understand its owners and constraints. Start with a limited change, measure whether it helps, and include the people who do the work. An improvement that creates hidden effort for another team is not an improvement. Make adoption and maintenance part of the proposal.

## Habits that matter throughout

Make commitments carefully. A new employee often wants to prove eagerness by saying yes immediately. A better pattern is to restate the request, check the constraints, and commit to a date you can defend. If you must renegotiate, do it before the deadline with a revised plan. Reliability is built from accurate promises and early communication, not from never encountering difficulty.

Separate facts, assumptions, and interpretations in your notes and updates. "The deployment was delayed two days" is a fact. "The delay was caused by inadequate testing" is an interpretation that needs evidence. This distinction helps a new employee learn without turning incomplete information into a judgment about colleagues.

Use meetings for decisions, discussion, relationship work, or problems that are easier to resolve live. Send the context and desired outcome beforehand. Afterward, record the decision, owner, and deadline where the team expects to find it. This discipline is especially valuable in remote teams, where people may otherwise reconstruct an important choice from fragments of chat.

Learn the organization's approach to security, privacy, and incident handling before you touch sensitive systems or customer data. In Web3 work, learn who controls production keys, how contract changes are authorized, where security findings go, and what the communications plan is for an incident. Curiosity is welcome; improvising around access controls is not.

Take care not to confuse visibility with contribution. Speaking often, scheduling many introductory calls, and joining every working group can look active while leaving core work untouched. Choose the relationships and forums necessary for your responsibilities, then create time for concentrated work. Your manager should be able to name the outcomes you own, not only the meetings you attended.

## Leave a usable handoff behind

By day 90, another person should be able to understand the work you own without searching through private notes. Create or improve a concise page that states the purpose, current status, key contacts, recurring decisions, important links, risks, and next dates. Keep it where the team already looks for this material. A handoff page is useful even when you are not changing roles; it reduces the cost of an absence and lets collaborators correct your understanding.

Use that page in the 90-day discussion. Confirm what you will continue to own, what you will support, and what should return to another team. If a responsibility is ambiguous, name the decision needed to settle it rather than carrying an indefinite promise. The period has done its job when your responsibilities, operating rhythm, and next commitments are understood by both you and your manager.

The first 90 days do not establish permanent mastery. They establish a working contract: colleagues know how to rely on you, you know how decisions and work move through the organization, and your manager has agreed priorities for what comes next. That is a stronger foundation than an attempt to impress everyone in the first week.
