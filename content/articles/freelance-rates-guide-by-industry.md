There is no universal freelance rate card. A developer fixing a production incident, a writer creating a regulated white paper, and a designer producing a small marketing graphic may all charge by the hour, day, project, or retainer, yet the numbers cannot be compared without scope. Public rate surveys can be useful prompts, but they are not a price list for your business. They often mix countries, experience levels, client types, and billing models.

Set a rate from the work you can describe and the income your practice needs to produce. Then test it in real proposals. A rate that covers only the hours spent doing visible client work will eventually fail because a freelancer also spends time finding work, preparing proposals, invoicing, maintaining tools, learning, handling contracts, and taking leave. In the United States, the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) generally treats people carrying on a trade or business as self-employed, with separate income-tax and self-employment-tax obligations. Rules vary by country and business structure, so seek local professional advice for your situation.

This article gives a method for setting and discussing rates. It intentionally does not publish a made-up range by industry. A number that is reasonable for a senior specialist serving a regulated enterprise can be unworkable for a new generalist serving local small businesses, even within the same occupation.

## Calculate the floor before quoting work

Start with your desired annual personal compensation, then add annual business costs and a margin for tax, leave, and uncertainty. Business costs may include software, devices, insurance, accounting, a coworking desk, marketing, subcontractors, payment processing, continuing education, and legal review. The amount is not a prediction of tax owed. It is a planning figure that keeps you from spending every payment as if it were salary.

Next, estimate billable capacity. A full-time employee may be paid for all working hours, but a solo freelancer cannot normally invoice every hour of a year. Sales calls, project planning, administration, proposal writing, sick days, holidays, gaps between projects, and unpaid revisions all reduce billable capacity. Be conservative in the first estimate. A planned 1,000 billable hours is more useful than an optimistic 1,800 that forces you to discount every proposal later.

The basic calculation is:

```text
annual revenue target / planned billable hours = minimum hourly equivalent
```

Suppose your annual revenue target is $120,000 and you can bill 1,000 hours. Your minimum hourly equivalent is $120 before considering a premium for scarce expertise, risk, urgency, or the value of the result. That does not mean every job should be billed at $120 per hour. It means a fixed-price project that takes twenty hours and returns $1,200 produces only $60 per hour before costs, which may not support the business you are trying to run.

Review the calculation at least quarterly during the first year. Compare planned and actual billable hours, expenses, unpaid work, average collection time, and effective hourly rate by project. If you consistently need twice as long as an estimate, the problem may be scope definition, workflow, skill, or rate. The answer is not automatically "work faster."

## Price the assignment, not the job title

Industry labels are too broad to set a price. "Writer" could mean editing a founder's 600-word draft, interviewing five subject-matter experts for a technical report, or writing regulated consumer copy. "Designer" could mean adapting one existing asset or defining an application design system. "Developer" could mean resolving a CSS issue, owning an on-call migration, or building a security-sensitive smart-contract integration.

Before naming a number, learn the commercial facts:

- What outcome does the client need, and how will they judge it?
- What is in scope, and what is expressly out of scope?
- Who supplies copy, data, access, approvals, brand material, and subject expertise?
- Who makes final decisions, and how many review rounds are included?
- What existing systems, dependencies, or compliance requirements affect the work?
- What date matters, and what happens when a client approval arrives late?
- Does the client need a transfer of intellectual-property rights, ongoing support, exclusivity, or travel?

Turn the answers into a short written scope. This protects both parties. A fixed price with undefined revisions is not a fixed price; it is an open commitment. A clear proposal can state the deliverables, assumptions, timeline, included meetings, review process, client responsibilities, change procedure, payment schedule, and ownership terms. Have a qualified local lawyer review a reusable agreement if you can. Contract enforceability and IP rules vary by jurisdiction.

## Writing and content work

Writing rates differ most by research burden, subject expertise, review complexity, and the business use of the finished work. A short article based on supplied material is not comparable to a customer case study that requires interviews, transcription, fact checking, executive review, and permission from the customer. A security or financial-services article may also require careful source work and a subject expert's approval.

Estimate the entire assignment. Include discovery, outline, research, interviews, drafting, revisions, formatting, and project communication. If the client supplies a detailed brief and accepts one review round, you can quote a tighter fixed price. If they ask for a thought-leadership program without a defined audience or point of view, offer a paid discovery phase before committing to a production price.

Specify whether your fee includes strategy, search optimization, publication in a CMS, image sourcing, fact checking, newsletter adaptation, or social posts. These are different services. Clarify rights as well. A client may need a work-for-hire arrangement or an assignment of rights; a portfolio use permission may matter to you. Do not assume that payment alone answers ownership questions.

For recurring content, a monthly retainer can work when the client needs a predictable production slot and you can define capacity. State the number and kind of deliverables, response times, meeting allowance, unused-capacity policy, and overage rate. A retainer is payment for agreed availability and output, not an invitation to send unlimited requests.

## Design work

Design pricing depends on the design problem and the decision process. A logo request can involve brand research, concept exploration, stakeholder interviews, typography choices, usage guidelines, and file preparation. A product-design request can involve research, flows, prototypes, accessibility review, handoff, and collaboration with engineers. Quoting both as "a design" hides the actual work.

Define the artifacts. For a marketing site, list the page types, breakpoints, components, copy status, prototype depth, and handoff format. For a brand assignment, identify the intended uses, number of initial directions, revision rounds, final file formats, and whether brand guidelines are included. For product work, agree on whether you are responsible for research, information architecture, interaction design, visual design, usability testing, design-system changes, or implementation support.

Price the review process as carefully as the screen count. A team with one empowered reviewer can move quickly. A committee that collects feedback from ten people may need scheduled review windows and a named decision maker. If the client changes the brief after you begin, use a change order with the revised scope, fee, and date. Good design work often reveals new questions; a change procedure lets those questions be handled without conflict.

Accessibility is part of scope when a design will be implemented. The [W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) provide a primary reference for criteria such as contrast, keyboard access, and text alternatives. A freelancer should not promise legal compliance unless the engagement, testing, jurisdiction, and authority to make implementation changes support that promise. Instead, state what review you will perform and what remains for the development team.

## Development and technical work

Technical work needs a risk premium when a mistake can interrupt service, expose data, or move assets. Start with the system boundary. Is the engagement a prototype, a feature in an existing application, a migration, a debugging assignment, a security review, or on-call support? The right price and contract for each differ.

Estimate discovery separately when the codebase is unfamiliar. A client asking for a "small fix" may have an undocumented deployment path, outdated dependencies, missing tests, or an issue outside the code they first identified. A short paid investigation can produce a better statement of work: what you found, possible approaches, expected effort, risks, and a recommendation. It also gives the client a useful artifact if they choose not to proceed.

For a build, define repository access, environments, acceptance tests, deployment responsibility, monitoring, documentation, support period, and ownership of infrastructure accounts. Identify dependencies the client controls, such as API credentials, DNS, app-store accounts, cloud billing, or a third-party vendor. If any of these are missing, the schedule should pause rather than make the freelancer responsible for an impossible deadline.

Smart-contract work deserves unusually explicit boundaries. The [Solidity documentation](https://docs.soliditylang.org/en/latest/security-considerations.html) cautions that users of smart contracts must consider security even when code is publicly available. A development engagement is not an audit, a formal verification engagement, or an insurance policy unless it expressly includes those services. Specify compiler version, target chain, upgrade model, roles, test requirements, audit expectations, deployment authority, and emergency procedures. Never charge a low fixed fee in exchange for an implied promise that code holding value is safe.

For maintenance, consider a prepaid block of hours or a monthly availability retainer with a defined response window. Distinguish business-hours support from incident response. A rate that includes a possible overnight production incident should account for the interruption and standby obligation.

## Marketing and growth work

Marketing assignments are prone to vague goals because the client wants a business result such as "more leads" or "better search performance." Those outcomes depend on product-market fit, sales follow-up, website speed, offer quality, competition, advertising spend, seasonality, and the client's own approval speed. Avoid guaranteeing a ranking, traffic figure, revenue amount, or follower count unless you control the relevant variables and have an enforceable basis for the guarantee.

Price research, planning, execution, reporting, and optimization separately or make each explicit in a monthly scope. A search engagement might include a technical crawl, keyword research, content recommendations, on-page implementation, reporting, and a working session with developers. Paid-media work might include account structure, creative briefs, campaign setup, bid management, reporting, and budget coordination. The advertising budget is normally the client's cost, not your fee.

Use metrics that map to the assignment. If you are hired to repair analytics, success may be validated event tracking and a documented reporting view. If you are hired to write campaign copy, success may be approved assets delivered by a date. If you are hired to manage media, agree on the attribution source and the decision interval before interpreting results. The [Federal Trade Commission's advertising guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) is a useful U.S. starting point for claims and endorsements, though clients may have additional rules by industry and location.

## Choose a billing model that matches uncertainty

Hourly billing is appropriate when the scope is genuinely unknown, when the client needs flexible access to your time, or when you are investigating a problem. It makes changes visible: more work costs more. It also requires time records that describe work clearly enough for a client to understand the invoice. Do not turn time tracking into a minute-by-minute performance theater; use sensible categories and retain the detail needed for disputed work.

Day or weekly rates work for concentrated collaboration, workshops, interim coverage, and embedded team work. Define what a day includes, whether travel is billable, how cancellations work, and how partial days are handled. A weekly rate should still state an expected capacity and whether meetings outside normal hours count against it.

Fixed pricing works when the deliverable and approval path are well defined. Price the risk of ambiguity rather than pretending it does not exist. Take a deposit or milestone payment before work begins, then tie later payments to meaningful stages such as discovery approval, first delivery, and final handoff. Payment practices are governed by contract and local law; do not rely on a generic template for a cross-border relationship.

Value-based pricing can make sense when the client can explain the economic value of a result and you can connect your work to it. It is not a license to guess at a company's revenue. Ask about the decision, alternatives, cost of delay, and expected use. A value conversation may justify a higher fixed fee, but the scope and deliverables still need definition.

## Negotiate scope before discounting

When a client says the rate is too high, ask what budget and result they have in mind. You may be able to reduce the number of deliverables, remove a service, extend the timeline, replace meetings with written feedback, or divide the work into phases. Those are scope changes. A discount with unchanged scope silently lowers your effective rate and often increases the chance of rushed work.

You can also offer a smaller paid first engagement. A research sprint, design audit, technical diagnosis, or pilot article lets the client evaluate your work without asking you to donate discovery. Set the price and the output before beginning. Free speculative work may be appropriate only when you independently choose it and can afford the time; it should not become a standard condition of winning work.

Raise rates when your capacity, costs, expertise, or demand changes. Give existing clients notice and apply the new price to future work where your agreement permits. A long-standing client may deserve a transition period or a different arrangement, but that is a business choice, not a rule. Keep a record of proposals won and lost, effective hourly rates, unpaid scope expansion, and payment delays. Those records will teach you more about your pricing than a generic industry chart.

## Protect cash flow and records

Invoice promptly with the agreed description, amount, payment due date, payment instructions, tax treatment where relevant, and purchase-order information if the client requires it. Follow up before the due date, not only after it. For larger fixed projects, milestone payments lower the chance that you finance months of client work from personal savings.

Keep records of income and ordinary business expenses. The IRS says self-employed individuals generally file an annual return and may need to pay estimated taxes quarterly; it also describes records and forms for U.S. sole proprietors on its [self-employed tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center). If you work outside the United States, check the comparable authority in your jurisdiction. An accountant can help you choose a recordkeeping system and understand registration, indirect tax, and cross-border obligations.

Your rate is a business decision made from costs, capacity, scope, risk, and evidence from completed work. State it calmly, put the scope in writing, and change the scope when the budget changes. That is more durable than chasing a single number that claims to fit every freelancer in an industry.
