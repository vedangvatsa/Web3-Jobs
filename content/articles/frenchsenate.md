French senators rejected a proposal on Nov. 24 that would have required taxpayers to declare self-custodied crypto holdings worth at least 100,000 euros (about $109,600) to tax authorities, according to the chamber's [official abridged record](https://www.senat.fr/cra/s20261124/s20261124.pdf) of the sitting. The vote came as the Senate debated the revenue section of France's 2027 budget, and it was one of three crypto tax amendments defeated that day.

Senators voted down identical amendments from Pascal Savoldelli and Anne-Sophie Romagny that would have applied the disclosure rule when the combined value of crypto held in wallets outside a service provider reached at least 100,000 euros on Dec. 31, [Cointelegraph reported](https://cointelegraph.com/news/french-senate-rejects-disclosing-self-custodied-crypto-wallets-100k). The requirement had first been adopted by the National Assembly on Nov. 3, but it fell days later when lawmakers rejected the budget's entire revenue section on Nov. 4.

Budget Minister David Amiel opposed the wallet-reporting amendments, saying they would not enable authorities to track how crypto is used in money laundering or tax fraud. The Senate's finance committee followed the government's recommendation to reject them. Public accounts minister David Amiel gave a related answer during the sitting: he told senators that stablecoins are not money, arguing they have neither legal tender status nor the power to settle debts and that many fall short of prudential stability requirements, Unchained reported from the record.

Faustine Fleuret, global head of public affairs at lending protocol Morpho, told Cointelegraph that requiring a declaration of wallet holdings without a taxable event would amount to "placing a blanket suspicion on good-faith users." She said self-custodial wallet reporting would create "unequal treatment" compared with other assets such as jewelry or fine art, "whose mere holding is not subject to a reporting obligation in the absence of a taxable transaction."

The Senate also rejected a Savoldelli amendment that would have taxed gains on crypto conversions into stablecoins classified as electronic-money tokens under EU rules from Jan. 1, 2027. The National Assembly had passed a similar measure from MP Nicolas Sansu on Oct. 16, but that proposal also died when the revenue section was rejected. Senator Christine Lavarde, the finance committee's general rapporteur, asked the government whether stablecoins count as money, saying the answer decided whether the measure could work; the committee then sided with the government against the amendment, according to Unchained.

A third failed amendment would have extended France's exit tax to unrealized crypto gains when qualifying taxpayers moved their tax residence abroad, bringing crypto under the levy's existing 800,000-euro threshold and measuring gains across a holder's entire portfolio, including coins held abroad. Senator Pierre Barros defended the proposal, saying current rules tax an executive who moves abroad with 5 million euros of company stock on unrealized gains while a crypto holder of similar wealth leaves owing nothing. The Assembly rejected similar proposals on Oct. 30, and both the committee and the government opposed the amendment this time.

One crypto-related amendment remains pending. Senator Thomas Dossus's proposal would raise a fine for refusing tax authorities' information requests from 10,000 euros to 50,000 euros per request, covering financial institutions, crypto service providers and platform operators.

The Senate is scheduled to complete debate and vote on the budget's revenue section on Saturday, with Sunday available if needed, Cointelegraph reported. A vote on the full budget is scheduled for Dec. 9. All three amendments were tabled by the Communist, Republican, Citizen and Ecologist - Kanaky group in the Senate, with Savoldelli as lead sponsor, and the record gives no vote tallies for the crypto proposals, only that none was adopted, Unchained reported. On the wallet declaration, Lavarde said it reflected a recommendation of the Husson-Raynal report. Amiel pointed instead to the EU's DAC8 tax-transparency rules, which he said have been transposed into France's tax code and apply to professionals involved in transactions between crypto users, arguing that a purely declarative obligation would not let authorities follow how crypto is used in money laundering or tax fraud cases.

The rejections leave France's current crypto tax rules untouched for now, including the deferral of tax on crypto-to-crypto trades that today covers swaps into stablecoins. Senator Grégory Blanc urged colleagues to back the stablecoin amendment, but the government and the Senate's finance committee opposed all three.
