Bitcoin mining pool operator Braiins published a [security report](https://braiins.com/blog/bitcoin-hashrate-hijacking-unencrypted-stratum-v1) on Nov. 25 warning that attackers have been rerouting traffic between miners and their pools to steal computing power. On Nov. 20, the report says, more than a thousand mining sites lost their pool connections in the same second. [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/braiins-warns-bitcoin-mining-attacks-affecting-1000-sites) covered the warning the same day.

The Nov. 20 wave was the largest so far. Braiins logged four separate episodes that day, mostly in Europe, each hitting between 1,050 and 1,600 mining sites and lasting between 6 and 49 minutes. The computing power pulled into a single episode ranged from 0.6 to 1.9 exahashes per second. Across the whole period from Nov. 7 to Nov. 20, the company counted 24 incidents, and the amount of computing power affected in one attack grew almost elevenfold over the two weeks.

The method does not require breaking into a machine or a pool. Stratum V1, the protocol most miners use to talk to pools, was written in 2012 and sends everything in plain text, so a miner has no way to check that the party answering on the other end is really its pool. The attacker exploited that by forging a route on the Border Gateway Protocol, the system networks use to announce which addresses they can reach. On Nov. 20 the legitimate pool address block held 4,096 addresses, and somebody announced a smaller block of 256 addresses that included part of it. Because the smaller announcement is more specific, routers that accepted it sent the miners' traffic to the attacker.

DNS was not touched. The miners reconnected within a second to the same hostname and the same IP address, where a different server answered the handshake in about 30 milliseconds instead of the roughly 280 milliseconds the real server needed, and accepted any username and password. That server handed out real bitcoin mining jobs and raised the share difficulty step by step, in one log reaching 2,147,483,640. The machines kept hashing at full speed while the pool dashboard showed nothing. A miner in Brazil that recorded a full episode on Nov. 16 showed the jobs came from a different large public pool, with that pool's tag and payout addresses in the coinbase, which told Braiins the attacker was running a proxy with its own worker account and submitting the stolen shares there.

The earlier wave was smaller and moved between continents. Between Nov. 7 and Nov. 12 the attacks hit Latin America, mostly Brazil and Argentina, in 19 episodes of 120 to 400 sites each, drawing 36 to 174 petahashes per second for periods of 1.5 minutes to 3 hours. A single Brazilian episode on Nov. 16 was captured packet by packet. Braiins read the pattern in one line: the episodes "were short, but they grew from under 175 PH/s to 1.9 EH/s in two weeks," and the company said it interprets the shift as the attacker testing the waters.

Braiins said neither its pool nor the affected machines were compromised. Pool accounts, balances and payout settings were not affected, and the attack plays out only on the network path between miner and pool. The company has not disclosed how much money miners may have lost. Miners may not notice at all, because the machines report normal hashrate and a live pool, so the drop shows up only on the pool dashboard while electricity keeps getting burned.

The weakness itself is old. Stratum V1 has carried mining traffic in clear text since 2012, and in 2014 Dell SecureWorks documented an attacker who used the same routing trick to take about $83,000 in mining rewards. Braiins said the vast majority of Bitcoin's computing power still uses unsecured Stratum V1 connections. Bitcoin's total hashrate stood at an estimated 1,016 exahashes per second on Nov. 20 according to MineOrNot data cited by [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/braiins-warns-bitcoin-mining-attacks-affecting-1000-sites), which put the largest single attack at roughly 0.19% of that total.

Fixes exist and are shipping. On the same day as the report, Braiins released a free proxy that speaks Stratum V1 to local miners and Stratum V2 to the pool, and pushed Braiins OS 26.11, which adds TLS with certificate and hostname verification and blocks fallbacks to plain text. When Braiins checked the public endpoints of the largest pools on Nov. 23, most still offered only plain-text Stratum V1, and lab tests of seven firmware versions on Nov. 20 and Nov. 23 found two widely used firmwares that accept invalid TLS certificates, which leaves the connection encrypted but still usable by whoever answers. Braiins urges miners to move to Stratum V2 or TLS, delete plain-text backup URLs, and alert on share difficulty above their pool's ceiling.

The incident follows a separate mining attack earlier in the month. [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/luxor-ceo-warns-of-ai-dark-forest-after-bitcoin-miner-hijack) reported on Nov. 16 that attackers exploited a weakness in Luxor Technology's mining management software, Commander, to redirect a little over a dozen customer accounts to the OCEAN pool. Luxor said it fixed the issue within two hours of detecting the unauthorized changes and promised to compensate the affected customers.

The forged route on Nov. 20 appeared four times that day before the address block owner's provider blocked the network that had propagated it that evening, and Braiins said the route has not returned since. Networks that validate routes against the official registry of address owners rejected the announcement; Braiins said the ones that do not validate were the ones left open. As a tell for operators, Braiins Pool never sets share difficulty above 16,777,216, so higher values in a miner's log mean the machine is no longer talking to the real pool.
