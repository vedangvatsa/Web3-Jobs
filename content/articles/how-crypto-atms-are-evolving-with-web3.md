A crypto ATM, also called a Bitcoin ATM or BTM, is a retail kiosk that lets a customer buy cryptocurrency with cash and, less commonly, sell cryptocurrency for cash. It is not a bank ATM connected to a checking account. The operator quotes a price, accepts cash or cryptocurrency, and arranges a blockchain transfer. The machine may be in a convenience store, gas station, or supermarket, but the store hosting it is often not the company running it.

Crypto ATMs can suit a person who has a specific, lawful reason to obtain a small amount of cryptocurrency with cash and understands the cost. They are a poor choice for anyone responding to an unexpected call, text, pop-up, or social-media message. The Federal Trade Commission's [consumer guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/09/scammers-use-bitcoin-atms-steal-your-money) says no legitimate business or government agency will tell someone to use a Bitcoin ATM to protect money or fix an account problem; that instruction is a fraud signal, not a security procedure.

## What the machine does

The word "ATM" can obscure the transaction. A conventional bank ATM dispenses money from an account. A crypto ATM usually performs a cash-to-crypto purchase from the operator or its liquidity provider. The customer is not depositing dollars into a personal blockchain account, and the kiosk is not automatically creating a Web3 identity.

The exact screen flow differs by operator and jurisdiction, but a purchase commonly has these steps:

1. The customer selects an asset and enters or scans a receiving address. This is normally a QR code from a wallet app. The address must match both the intended asset and network. For example, a Bitcoin address cannot receive an Ethereum token.
2. The machine presents a quoted exchange rate and its fees. It may also state a cash limit, identity-verification requirement, and estimated delivery time.
3. The customer inserts cash and completes any required verification. Depending on the amount and the operator's compliance program, that can include a phone number, government-issued identification, a photo, or other information.
4. The operator accepts the order and sends cryptocurrency from a company-controlled wallet or instructs a provider to send it. The customer should receive a receipt showing the asset, amount, exchange rate, fees, destination address, time, and transaction identifier if one is available.
5. The network processes the transfer. A wallet may show an incoming transaction before it has enough confirmations to be considered final. Confirmation timing varies with the blockchain and its current conditions.

For a sale, the order is reversed. The customer sends cryptocurrency to an address or QR code supplied by the operator, waits for the required confirmations, and then obtains cash if the kiosk supports cash-out. A customer should not send coins until they have read the operator's terms and confirmed the amount of cash to be paid. A screenshot of a QR code, a pending-wallet display, or a machine receipt is not the same as a completed and settled transaction.

The receiving address deserves unusual care. Blockchains generally do not have a chargeback mechanism. If a customer sends cryptocurrency to the wrong address, to the wrong network, or to a scammer's address, the operator may be unable to reverse it. Before inserting cash, compare the first and last characters of the address on the machine with the address in the wallet. A small test transaction can reduce the consequence of an address mistake, though it also adds cost and is not practical for every purchase.

## Price, spread, and fees

The dollar price displayed at a kiosk is not necessarily the market price shown on a cryptocurrency website. A crypto ATM's cost can include an explicit service fee, a spread between the operator's quoted rate and a reference market rate, a network fee, or several of these items. A screen that advertises a low fee can still quote an unfavorable exchange rate, so the useful comparison is the final amount of cryptocurrency the customer will receive for a given amount of cash.

Ask four questions before committing: How many dollars will leave my hand? How much cryptocurrency will arrive at the stated address? Which network will carry it? What is the full cost relative to an independently checked reference price at that moment? Keep the receipt and take a photo of the final disclosure if the operator permits it.

Costs are not the only trade-off. Cash is immediate, but a crypto transfer can remain pending, and a kiosk's availability does not make it cheaper or safer than other lawful ways to buy cryptocurrency. A customer who does not need cash access should compare the total price, identity requirements, withdrawal rules, and customer support offered by regulated services available where they live. No provider can remove the market risk of the asset itself: the value received may fall after purchase.

## Why identification and limits appear

Crypto ATMs are commercial financial services, not anonymous portals. In the United States, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network's (FinCEN) [2019 guidance on convertible virtual currency](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20Guidance%20CVC%20FINAL%20508.pdf) explains that a person who accepts and transmits convertible virtual currency, or buys or sells it as a business, can be a money transmitter under FinCEN rules. The guidance specifically treats a CVC kiosk owner or operator that accepts currency from a customer and transmits CVC as a money transmitter.

That classification can require registration as a money services business, an anti-money-laundering program, recordkeeping, reporting, and customer-identification controls. State money-transmission laws can impose additional licensing and operational requirements. The details depend on the operator's model, transaction amount, location, and applicable law; a kiosk label alone does not establish that every operator has the same obligations.

For customers, compliance can mean a transaction is paused for review or declined, a higher purchase limit triggers additional identification, or a cash-out transaction is delayed until a blockchain transfer receives enough confirmations. It can also mean the operator retains personal information. Read the privacy notice before providing an ID or biometric image. A legitimate compliance check does not make an investment safe, and it does not justify sending funds to a stranger.

If a machine claims that it needs no identity checks at any amount, promises guaranteed returns, or asks a customer to bypass its stated rules, treat that as a reason to leave. Verify the operator's legal name, support contact, and terms through its official website, not a phone number supplied by someone urging the transaction. Consumers can also check their state financial regulator for licensing information and complaints where that information is available.

## The fraud pattern to recognize

Crypto ATM fraud usually begins away from the machine. The FTC describes government impersonation, business impersonation, and tech-support scams in which a caller or message creates urgency, tells the target to withdraw cash, and directs the target to scan a QR code at a kiosk. That QR code supplies the scammer's wallet address. Once cash is inserted, the purchased cryptocurrency is sent to the scammer.

The scale is not theoretical. The FTC [reported](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/new-ftc-data-shows-massive-increase-losses-bitcoin-atm-scams) that consumers said they lost more than $110 million to scams involving Bitcoin ATMs in 2023, nearly ten times the amount reported in 2020. It also reported more than $65 million in reported losses in the first half of 2024, with consumers over 60 more than three times as likely as younger adults to report a loss. Those figures are reports to the FTC, not a count of every loss, but they show why a kiosk transaction prompted by another person needs a hard stop.

Common pressure tactics include claims that a bank account has been compromised, a government agency has issued a warrant, a computer needs urgent repair, or a romantic contact needs help. A scammer may tell the customer to keep the purchase secret, stay on the phone at the kiosk, lie about the purpose of the transaction, or scan a QR code from a message. None of those instructions protects funds. They are attempts to route an irreversible payment to the scammer.

Do not use a crypto ATM because someone tells you to. End the call or conversation, do not scan their code, and contact the purported bank, company, or agency through a phone number or website independently obtained by you. The FTC's [consumer alert](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/09/scammers-use-bitcoin-atms-steal-your-money) advises consumers never to withdraw cash in response to an unexpected call or message and to report fraud at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). If money has already been sent, preserve the receipt, wallet address, transaction hash, messages, and phone numbers, notify the kiosk operator promptly, contact local law enforcement, and make a report. Recovery is uncertain, so speed and records matter.

## What a crypto ATM has to do with Web3

Web3 is a broad label for applications and systems that use blockchain networks, wallet-based accounts, smart contracts, and sometimes decentralized governance. A crypto ATM may be an on-ramp: it can send an asset to an address that the customer controls and that address may later be used with a wallet-based application. That limited connection is real.

It does not make the kiosk itself decentralized. The operator sets its offered assets, exchange rate, fees, cash limits, verification process, and support policies. It relies on retail hardware, banking relationships, custody or liquidity arrangements, and compliance operations. Those are centralized service decisions. The blockchain transfer may settle on a public network, but the cash transaction that starts it is mediated by a company.

Nor does buying cryptocurrency at a kiosk automatically give a customer safe access to decentralized finance, staking, token swaps, or NFTs. Those activities can require a compatible wallet, the correct network's native token for transaction fees, careful review of smart-contract permissions, and an understanding of price and loss risks. Some kiosk operators may advertise additional services, but consumers should evaluate each service separately. A machine that can sell an asset is not evidence that a particular protocol, token, or NFT project is legitimate.

Self-custody creates another boundary. If the customer uses a wallet for which they alone hold the recovery phrase or private key, no ATM operator can restore access if that secret is lost or shared. Never enter a wallet recovery phrase into a kiosk, website, chat, or support form. A real wallet provider does not need it to receive cryptocurrency. Conversely, if an operator provides a hosted wallet or an account, the operator may control the withdrawal process and account access; read those custody terms before buying.

## A safer way to decide

Use a crypto ATM only after making the decision independently, not while someone watches or directs the process. Bring an address generated by your own wallet, confirm the asset and network, review the all-in quote before cash is accepted, and retain the receipt. Do not let another person provide the QR code or tell you which address to use.

For a first purchase, use an amount you can afford to lose through price movement, fees, a mistake, or fraud. Test that you can open the wallet and recognize its receiving address before visiting the kiosk. If the machine's quote, disclosure, identity request, or destination address is unclear, cancel rather than guessing. The machine will still be there after you have had time to verify the details.

## FAQ

### Are crypto ATMs anonymous?

No. Operators can require different levels of identification and can collect transaction records. U.S. anti-money-laundering requirements and state rules may apply to the operator. Do not confuse a cash-fed machine with anonymous cryptocurrency use.

### Can I get my money back after sending crypto from an ATM?

Usually not through a normal chargeback process. Ask the operator for help immediately and report suspected fraud, but blockchain transfers to a scammer's wallet may be irreversible. The FTC says a request to use a Bitcoin ATM to protect money or solve an account problem is a scam.

### Does a crypto ATM give me access to Web3?

It can fund a wallet address with a supported asset. It does not create a decentralized identity, validate a dApp, protect a recovery phrase, or make smart-contract activity safe. Those are separate choices and responsibilities.

### Are all crypto ATM fees the same?

No. Operators can charge different explicit fees and quote different exchange rates. Compare the final cryptocurrency amount and all disclosed charges, not only a headline fee percentage.
