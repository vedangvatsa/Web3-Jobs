A raise conversation is a request to align your pay with the work you are doing, the results you produce, and the pay range for comparable roles. It is not a test of whether you can make a persuasive speech in an unexpected moment. Good preparation gives your manager information they may not have, while good timing gives them a reasonable chance to act on it.

This guide is for employees who want to discuss a salary increase with their current employer, including people in remote, technical, operations, and Web3-adjacent roles. It focuses on a direct internal request. Negotiating a new-job offer has different constraints because an employer has not yet seen your work.

No script can create budget that does not exist. Pay decisions can involve a manager, department head, finance team, compensation group, and a fixed review cycle. Still, a specific request backed by records is easier to assess than a general statement that you work hard or need more money.

## Build a case before you ask

Start a record several weeks before the meeting. Your manager may remember a recent problem more vividly than work you completed six months ago. A concise document corrects that imbalance and makes it easier for them to explain your request to people who were not in the room.

List accomplishments from the period since your last pay discussion. For each item, write the situation, your action, the result, and evidence that supports the result. Keep the language factual. A useful entry might read:

- Reduced the weekly customer-onboarding backlog from 42 accounts to 11 by documenting the handoff process and training two teammates; dashboard data covers April through June.
- Took ownership of incident communications during the migration; sent status updates, coordinated engineering and support, and helped close 18 tickets within the agreed response time.
- Delivered the partner reporting workflow two weeks before the committed date; the finance team now receives the report without manual spreadsheet cleanup.

The relevant results vary by job. Sales staff can point to revenue, renewals, pipeline quality, or margin. Engineers can cite reliability, delivery against an agreed plan, security work, reduced support burden, or an internal system adopted by other teams. Designers can connect work to completed launches, usability findings, conversion changes, or a faster review process. Operations, community, support, and program staff can document cycle time, volume, service levels, cost avoidance, risk reduction, and stakeholder feedback.

Do not inflate a number or claim sole credit for team work. State your part: “I designed the rollout plan and trained the support team” is stronger and more credible than “I made the launch successful.” If a result cannot be measured in dollars, use another verifiable measure such as time, volume, defect rate, adoption, deadline, or scope. A manager can ask follow-up questions; bring the source data or links to dashboards when appropriate.

Also document changes in the role itself. Compare your current responsibilities with the job description, goals set at your last review, or the work normally expected at your level. A sustained increase in scope is often more relevant than one unusually busy week. Examples include managing a project across functions, mentoring new hires, owning a recurring business process, handling a larger customer segment, or doing work associated with the next level.

Personal expenses, inflation, and a long commute may explain why more income matters to you, but they do not show why an employer should change your compensation. Keep the conversation centered on the job, demonstrated contribution, and the market for that work.

## Research a defensible salary range

Market data is context, not a verdict. Job titles are inconsistent, and published figures can mix seniority, location, base salary, bonuses, and equity. Use several sources and compare roles with similar responsibilities, experience, location, and company stage.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes occupational wage estimates through its [Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program](https://www.bls.gov/oes/). Its data can provide a broad local or national reference point for established occupations. The BLS also publishes the [Employment Cost Index](https://www.bls.gov/eci/), which tracks changes in employer compensation costs over time. These sources are useful for understanding the wider pay environment, but they will not capture every specialty, token-based component, or startup-stage role.

For a narrower view, look at reputable salary surveys, job postings that disclose ranges, recruiter conversations, and compensation pages for comparable employers. Some U.S. jurisdictions require pay-range disclosure in job advertisements. A posting is an advertised range, not proof that every employee in that role receives the top number, so record the date, location, level, and job requirements before treating it as a comparison.

For roles with equity, tokens, commission, bonuses, or variable pay, separate those components. Decide whether your request concerns base salary, total cash compensation, title and level, or the full package. A higher target figure can be misleading if one number includes a bonus and another does not. If a grant or token award has vesting, liquidity, or valuation uncertainty, do not treat its headline value as the same as cash compensation.

Choose three numbers before the meeting: your requested salary, a range you believe is reasonable, and the lowest outcome that would make staying make sense for you. You do not need to disclose the last number. The requested salary should be a precise, considered figure rather than “as much as possible.” If you are paid below comparable roles and your responsibilities have expanded, explain both facts. If you are seeking a routine merit increase, a modest request consistent with company practice may be more realistic.

Research also helps you avoid a common mistake: asking for a percentage without knowing the resulting salary. A 5% increase has very different consequences at different starting salaries. State the annual base amount you are requesting, then use a percentage only as a secondary reference if it helps.

## Choose timing that works with the company process

Ask your manager how compensation decisions are made if you do not already know. Many employers set budgets and approvals well before annual reviews. A request made after the budget is finalized may still be valid, but it can require an exception or a later effective date. Knowing the calendar lets you raise the subject early enough for your manager to include it in planning.

Performance reviews, promotion cycles, and the completion of a substantial project can all be appropriate occasions. A recent achievement is helpful when it reflects sustained work, not when it is the only evidence you have. Schedule a dedicated 30-minute meeting instead of trying to raise the subject in a standup, a social event, or the last two minutes of a one-on-one.

The company’s financial condition matters. During layoffs, a hiring freeze, missed targets, or a cash-conservation period, an immediate increase may be unlikely. That does not require silence. You can ask for a candid explanation of the process, the criteria for a future adjustment, and a date to revisit the discussion. Do not interpret a difficult quarter as a judgment on your performance without asking.

Avoid treating another job offer as a routine negotiating device. An outside offer is relevant only if it is real, in writing, and you are prepared to accept it. Some employers will not counteroffer; others may view the disclosure as notice that you plan to leave. Never invent an offer or imply one exists. The conversation should stand on your internal record even if you have no external option.

## Request the meeting and make the ask

Keep the invitation simple. For example:

> Could we set aside 30 minutes this week to discuss my role, my recent results, and my compensation? I will send a short summary in advance.

Sending a one-page summary before the meeting can help a busy manager prepare. It should contain your main accomplishments, added responsibilities, market context, and requested salary. Do not attach a long folder of raw work unless your manager asks for it. The goal is to make the decision easy to understand, not to create a document no one reads.

In the meeting, lead with the subject rather than circling around it. You can use this structure:

> I would like to discuss adjusting my base salary to $[amount]. Since my last compensation review, I have taken on [responsibility] and [responsibility]. I also [specific result], [specific result], and [specific result]. Based on that scope and the market information I reviewed for comparable [level] roles in [location], I believe $[amount] is an appropriate base salary. What is the process and timeline for considering this request?

Use your own words. The point is to make a clear request, give the evidence, and ask for a process. Then stop talking. A pause gives the manager room to respond. Filling it with apologies, concessions, or unrelated personal details can weaken the clarity of the request.

Stay calm if the manager asks for time or questions an example. A useful response is, “I can send the dashboard and project summary after this meeting.” If they say the figure is outside the range, ask which range, level, or policy applies and whether your current responsibilities match that level. Questions are more productive than arguing over an unsupported number.

Be careful with comparisons to colleagues. Pay differences can have many causes, and you may not know the full facts. Say, “My research indicates that comparable roles are paid in this range,” rather than naming a coworker or repeating private compensation information. In the United States, the [National Labor Relations Board](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/the-law/employees/your-right-to-discuss-wages) explains that most private-sector employees have a right to discuss wages, subject to important exceptions. That right does not make a coworker’s pay a complete case for your own increase.

## Handle yes, delay, and no professionally

A yes should produce details. Ask for the new base salary, effective date, pay period, title or level if relevant, and written confirmation. Thank your manager, but do not assume a verbal statement has completed payroll or approval steps. A short follow-up email can confirm what both of you understood.

“I need to check” is neither a yes nor a no. Ask what approval is needed and when you should expect an update. A practical follow-up is: “Thank you for looking into this. Can we set 15 minutes on the calendar for [date] to review the decision?” Put the date in writing. Without a date, a request can disappear behind more urgent work.

If the answer is no, separate the reason from the emotion of the moment. Ask direct questions:

- Is the issue budget, company policy, my current level, performance, or the requested amount?
- What specific outcomes or responsibilities would support an adjustment?
- Who makes the decision, and what must they see?
- When is the next compensation review or budget window?
- Can we write down the goals and schedule a follow-up meeting now?

A manager who cannot approve a raise may still be able to explain the path. Ask for measurable expectations. “Show more leadership” is too vague to manage. “Lead the next partner integration, keep the launch on schedule, and document the operating process by the end of Q2” creates criteria you can revisit. After the meeting, send a short recap: the request, the feedback, agreed goals, owner, and review date. This is not confrontational; it prevents two people from remembering a vague conversation differently.

If the explanation is a budget constraint, do not accept an indefinite promise. Ask whether an increase can be effective at the next cycle, whether a promotion review can happen separately, and what evidence is needed. Continue documenting your work. If the company repeatedly postpones a clear answer or changes the criteria after you meet them, that is useful information for deciding whether to pursue other roles.

## Consider alternatives without losing sight of base pay

Base salary affects predictable income and may influence future offers, bonuses, and benefits calculations. It is reasonable to keep it as the primary request. When a base increase is not possible, alternatives can address a real need or recognize a changed role, but they should be specific and documented.

Possible alternatives include a one-time bonus, a defined promotion review, a title correction, additional equity subject to its terms, a retention payment, a training budget, extra paid time off, a flexible schedule, remote-work support, or a revised commission plan. Each has trade-offs. A one-time bonus does not raise recurring pay. Equity may have no near-term liquidity. A title without changed pay may help future job searches but does not cover current expenses. Extra flexibility can be valuable, but it should not be used to hide an unsustainable workload.

Ask for the terms in writing: amount, tax treatment if known, vesting or repayment conditions, effective date, eligibility rules, and what happens if you change roles or leave. If an employer offers an alternative, take time to review it rather than agreeing in the meeting. You can say, “Thank you. I would like to review the written details before I decide.”

## Follow through after the conversation

Send a brief email within a day. If the request is being reviewed, summarize the requested amount, the evidence discussed, and the agreed decision date. If goals were set, list them in plain language. Keep a copy of the email and continue the accomplishment record until the next meeting.

Do not make a salary conversation a single annual event. Use regular one-on-ones to confirm priorities, surface changes in scope, and ask for feedback while there is time to act on it. This makes a future compensation discussion less surprising because your manager has already seen the work and the expectations.

If the outcome does not meet your needs, decide privately what you will do next. Updating your resume, speaking with recruiters, or applying elsewhere can be sensible career management. Do it because you want to assess your options, not as a threat. A professional departure can preserve relationships; an ultimatum often narrows them.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much of a raise should I ask for?

There is no universal percentage. Start with the base salary justified by your role, scope, location, performance, and comparable market data. The BLS Employment Cost Index can show broad wage movement, while role-specific sources can help establish a range. Request an amount you can explain with evidence instead of selecting a percentage because it sounds conventional.

### Should I ask for a raise after six months?

Possibly. Six months can be enough when you were hired below the role’s range, your responsibilities changed materially, or you completed work that clearly exceeds the original role. In other cases, wait for the employer’s review cycle while documenting results. Ask your manager about the compensation calendar rather than guessing.

### What if my manager says there is no budget?

Ask when budgets are set, whether the request can be considered in the next cycle, and what written goals would support it. Set a follow-up date. You can also discuss a promotion review or a defined alternative, but compare its terms with the base-pay request.

### Should I give an ultimatum?

Usually no. State an external offer only when you are ready to take it and understand that the employer may decline to match it. An ultimatum can end the discussion quickly. A clear internal case and a documented decision timeline are safer starting points.

### Do I need to disclose my salary history?

Rules vary by location. Some jurisdictions restrict employers from asking about salary history, while others do not. You can keep the discussion focused on the compensation you seek for this role and the evidence supporting it. Check the applicable local law or seek qualified advice for a legal question.
