An investment analyst at a [Web3](/what-is-web3) venture capital fund helps partners decide which founders and protocols deserve an investment. It is one of the more intellectually rewarding roles in cryptocurrency because the work crosses technical design, market questions, founder assessment, and written argument. The analyst's task is not to repeat a project's pitch. It is to establish whether the evidence supports a case for, or against, backing a project that may influence the decentralized internet.

The research environment differs from traditional finance, where analysis often relies on private company data and financial statements. Web3 analysts can examine open-source code and transparent on-chain metrics alongside founder claims and community activity. More visible information does not make the decision automatic. It gives the analyst more material to test, compare, and explain. For the wider range of roles around the discipline, see [how to get a job in Web3 venture capital](/web3-vc-jobs).

The role has three linked responsibilities: source projects before they receive widespread attention, lead diligence into their viability, and write the investment memo. Each step should improve the next. A lead found through ecosystem participation becomes a research question. Diligence turns that question into an evidence-based view. The memo gives partners a concise record on which to make a decision.

## Source projects before they are widely known

Sourcing means finding projects before they gain broad attention. It requires immersion in the crypto ecosystem: being active on Twitter, engaging in Discord communities, and attending hackathons to build rapport with founders and developers. These settings can expose an analyst to early work, technical discussion, and the people behind a project.

The point is not to treat social activity as validation. Twitter, Discord, and hackathons are places to develop leads and context, not substitutes for diligence. An analyst should be able to explain why a founder, protocol, or technical direction deserves further review. That requires listening closely, retaining the difference between a claim and evidence, and asking questions that can be followed through in the diligence process.

Rapport improves the quality of that work. A conversation with a founder or developer can give an analyst better questions about the project, while participation in a community can reveal what users and builders are discussing. The analyst still has to document the evidence independently. The value of sourcing is that it produces a focused research pipeline rather than a stream of unexamined announcements.

## Lead a disciplined diligence process

Diligence is the thorough investigation used to evaluate a project's viability. It covers the team, the protocol, the token, on-chain activity, and the market. These areas should be examined separately before they are joined in an investment thesis. A strong technical idea does not eliminate questions about the founders or token distribution; visible on-chain activity does not eliminate the need to identify competitors.

### Team analysis

Team analysis starts with identifying the founders and assessing their backgrounds. The fund is considering people as well as a protocol, so the analyst needs a grounded view of who is responsible for the project. The work should distinguish what is known about the founders from the analyst's interpretation of how that information bears on the investment case. Sourcing relationships can sharpen the questions, but diligence must rest on evidence rather than personal enthusiasm.

### Technical diligence

Technical diligence asks how the protocol functions and whether its architecture has integrity. Deep programming knowledge is not required for the analyst role, but the analyst must understand relevant technical concepts and be able to read code well enough to evaluate project risks. The standard is not to perform the work of a protocol engineer. It is to move beyond broad technical language, understand the design being presented, and explain why a technical observation matters to the investment thesis.

### Tokenomics analysis

[Tokenomics](/understanding-tokenomics) analysis examines the utility of the [token](/what-is-a-token) and the fairness of its distribution. An analyst should be able to state what the token is meant to do within the project, how it is distributed, and why those points matter. A token's existence is not an explanation of its purpose. Its utility and distribution need their own treatment in the diligence record.

### On-chain data analysis

On-chain analysis uses tools such as Dune and Nansen to assess a project's traction. Transparent on-chain metrics give the analyst evidence that can be examined alongside the project's public narrative. A metric or dashboard is not, by itself, an investment conclusion. The analyst should specify what question the data addresses, how it relates to the protocol or token, and what conclusion can reasonably follow from it.

### Market analysis

Market analysis estimates the total addressable market and identifies competitors. It places the protocol in the field it seeks to address rather than treating the project as if it has no alternatives. The analyst should explain the problem the project is intended to address, the competitive context, and the pressure that context places on the investment thesis. The aim is a clear basis for the opportunity, not a large but unsupported claim about it.

The five areas of diligence should converge in the final judgment. A project may look strong in one area and weak in another; the analyst's job is to make that tension explicit. The resulting view is more useful when it can explain how a team assessment, technical design, token distribution, on-chain traction, and competitive context support or challenge the same thesis. This is the difference between a collection of research notes and an investment analysis.

## Turn research into an investment memo

Investment memo writing is the point at which sourcing and diligence become a decision document. The memo encapsulates the findings of the investigation and must make a clear, concise, evidence-based argument for or against an investment. A partner should be able to follow the project, founders, technical design, tokenomics, on-chain analysis, and market context without having to reconstruct the analyst's reasoning from scattered notes.

The memo should distinguish observation from interpretation. It can argue for an investment while identifying risks and uncertainty. That is not a weakness in the document; it is evidence that the analyst is testing a thesis rather than producing promotion. Clear writing matters because the fund needs an argument it can inspect, challenge, and use in a decision.

## Skills that support the work

- **Insatiable curiosity.** A strong desire to learn and explore complex topics is essential. Projects can require an analyst to move among protocol design, token mechanics, on-chain activity, social discussion, and market structure. Curiosity is useful when it produces better questions and sustained research.

- **Analytical and critical thinking.** The analyst must synthesize technical, financial, and social information into a coherent investment thesis. That means explaining how evidence fits together, where it conflicts, and why the analyst reaches a particular view rather than collecting favorable observations.

- **Excellent written communication.** Investment memos need to be clear and persuasive without abandoning evidence. The same discipline applies to public research: a reader should be able to identify the question, the supporting material, and the conclusion.

- **Networking and social skills.** Building relationships with founders, developers, analysts, and associates is vital in venture capital. The useful form of networking is substantive: it improves understanding, creates room for informed questions, and invites feedback that strengthens the work.

- **Technical literacy.** The ability to understand technical concepts and read code is necessary to evaluate project risks, even though deep programming knowledge is not required. Technical literacy lets an analyst test architectural claims instead of relying only on social or market signals.

In practice, these skills act as checks on one another. Curiosity identifies a question, technical literacy helps frame it, analysis tests the evidence, writing makes the conclusion inspectable, and relationships improve the quality of the questions being asked. A candidate does not need to present a finished answer to every project. They need to show a repeatable method for moving from an observation to a well-supported view.

## Build public evidence of the skill set

Entry into crypto venture capital often does not follow a linear path. Candidates commonly demonstrate passion and analytical ability publicly before they hold an analyst title. Public work can serve as a resume because it lets others assess how a candidate frames a question, handles evidence, and communicates a conclusion.

1. **Become a "degen" and a user.** Gain first-hand experience with the products you hope to evaluate. This does not replace analysis or prove a project's quality. It gives the candidate a more direct understanding of the user experience behind a protocol's claims.

2. **Develop a focused public profile.** Establish expertise in a niche rather than trying to cover every subject at once. A blog, newsletter, or Twitter account can publish high-quality research on zero-knowledge proofs, MEV, or specific L1 ecosystems. Focus makes it easier for others to see a candidate's contribution.

3. **Build a [portfolio](/building-web3-portfolio) of public work.** Public analyses can demonstrate the same capabilities needed in an analyst role. Create insightful [Dune dashboards](/on-chain-analyst-job), write and share an investment thesis on a project you admire, and contribute actively to a [DAO](/what-is-a-dao)'s governance forum. A dashboard demonstrates on-chain analysis; a thesis demonstrates diligence and writing; governance participation shows engagement with a decentralized community.

4. **Network around the analysis.** Cultivate relationships with analysts and associates at funds you admire. Instead of merely asking for a job, ask for feedback on a specific analysis. This gives the conversation a concrete subject and demonstrates that the candidate is prepared to revise a view when evidence or reasoning changes.

Public work is most persuasive when it resembles the work the role requires. A dashboard should answer a defined question, not merely display data. A written thesis should distinguish evidence from conclusion. A governance contribution should show that the candidate can engage with an argument in a public setting. This gives potential colleagues a basis for assessing the candidate's research habits before an interview.

A career as a Web3 investment analyst is challenging and rewarding. It offers the opportunity to support founders building foundational infrastructure for the next web. The credible route into the role is similarly foundational: use the products, learn a focused area, publish careful work, seek substantive feedback, and show that the research can support a decision.
