Digital advertising functions as the backbone of the Web2 internet and is heavily influenced by major players such as Google and Meta. The sector generates billions in revenue through a model that relies on surveillance. Companies provide "free" services in exchange for the ability to track behavior, compile data profiles, and sell attention to advertisers. The rise of [Web3](/what-is-web3) challenges that arrangement by promoting user ownership, privacy, and decentralization.

The change under discussion is not simply a new format for displaying ads. It is a proposed change in who controls data, who grants permission to use it, and who receives value when attention is sold. In the conventional model, the platform gathers information and organizes the transaction between advertiser and audience. In a Web3-oriented model, the user is meant to have a more direct role in that exchange. That raises practical questions about consent, targeting, rewards, creator income, and usability.

Web3 does not remove the need for advertisers to reach relevant audiences or for publishers and creators to fund their work. It offers a different framework for those activities. Its promise is a system in which the user has more control over data and attention, while value can move more directly among users, advertisers, and creators. Whether that promise is realized depends on the quality of the experience as much as on the underlying technology.

## The problems in the Web2 model

The existing advertising framework has several structural weaknesses. The first is surveillance capitalism. Platforms track online activities to create detailed user profiles, leading to significant privacy invasions. The issue is not merely that advertising exists. It is that the business model depends on collecting behavioral information at a scale users may not fully see or control. When data collection is built into the exchange for a service, consent can become difficult to separate from participation.

Data ownership is a related problem. Users do not own their data; it resides with platforms. This allows a small number of corporations to dominate the market and suppress competition. The concentration matters because the platforms that control data also control much of the path between advertisers and audiences. A user may supply the attention and generate the behavior that becomes valuable, yet have little influence over how that information is used.

Transparency is another weak point in the advertising supply chain. Advertisers often cannot trace where their funds go, while publishers are unaware of their actual earnings. This lack of clarity makes it difficult for the parties involved to evaluate the exchange. An advertiser cannot easily see the complete route taken by its spending, and a publisher cannot always see how the amount it receives relates to the value produced by its audience. The system can continue to operate while leaving both sides with incomplete information.

The result is a model in which users are often treated as products rather than customers. Platforms prioritize engagement over user needs, producing misaligned interests. A user wants a service and some control over personal information. An advertiser wants relevant attention. A creator or publisher wants a fair way to fund work. A platform organized around data extraction and engagement can place its own incentives between all three. The central Web3 proposition is that the arrangement can be redesigned around consent and direct value exchange.

## A user-centered alternative

Web3 introduces an advertising framework centered on user control over data and attention. The model does not require advertisers to stop seeking an audience. It asks them to obtain access through clearer permission and to recognize that attention has value for the person providing it. The user is no longer only the input to a profiling system; the user is intended to be a participant in the exchange.

In this model, ownership and privacy are not separate topics. Control over data determines whether a person can decide what to disclose, while a privacy-focused approach determines how much information needs to be revealed for an advertiser to act. A system that requires the user to surrender unnecessary details would reproduce much of the problem it claims to solve. The relevant design goal is to share only what is needed for the chosen interaction.

The model also changes how advertising value can be described. Instead of treating a view or an audience profile as something a platform captures and resells, it allows for a direct arrangement in which an advertiser pays a user for permission to access data or display advertisements. Users dictate what data is shared and with whom. This is a meaningful shift in agency. The user can make a choice about participation rather than having that choice embedded invisibly in a service's default operation.

Such a framework still has to be understandable. A permission mechanism is only useful if a person can tell what they are agreeing to, what data is involved, and what they receive in return. The Web3 idea is therefore not complete at the level of a wallet or a token. It must appear as a usable exchange that respects the decision it asks the user to make.

## Wallet-based control and attention rewards

In the Web3 model, users maintain control over data through a **[crypto wallet](/how-to-choose-a-crypto-wallet)**. The wallet is presented as the point from which a user can manage participation in a privacy-focused advertising system. This approach reframes the relationship between identity, data, and advertising: the user should be able to decide whether access is granted instead of assuming that a platform controls the relevant information by default.

The mechanism is direct. Advertisers pay users for permission to access data or to display advertisements. Users dictate what data is shared and with whom. The significance of this arrangement lies in the permission itself. It creates a clearer connection between an advertiser's request and a user's choice. A user can decide whether the proposed exchange is worthwhile, and the advertiser can address a person who has chosen to participate.

The Brave browser is an example of this model. It blocks conventional ads and trackers by default. Users can choose to view privacy-oriented ads and receive Basic Attention [Token](/what-is-a-token) (BAT) rewards for their attention. The token can be used to support content creators or exchanged for other benefits. The example is useful because it makes the proposed value flow concrete: a user opts in, attention is recognized with a reward, and that reward can support a creator or be used in another way.

This does not make rewards a substitute for good advertising or good content. It changes the terms on which attention is requested. In the conventional surveillance model, attention is commonly monetized through profiling that happens around the user. In the wallet-based model, the user is meant to have a more visible choice and a share of the exchange. That is the aspect that aligns the advertising mechanism with Web3's emphasis on ownership and user control.

## Targeting without unnecessary disclosure

[Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs)](/zero-knowledge-proofs-explained) offer a way to think about targeted advertising without giving advertisers direct access to personal data. Targeting is often presented as the reason broad data collection is necessary: an advertiser wants to reach people with particular attributes, so it purchases or gathers information about those people. ZKPs challenge the assumption that the advertiser needs to see the raw details in order to use a relevant criterion.

Consider the example of an advertiser that wants to reach users over the age of 21 who live in California. In Web2, the advertiser would purchase this information from brokers. In the Web3 approach described here, a user can generate a ZKP with a decentralized identity that proves the user meets the criteria without disclosing their actual age or location. The advertiser receives the answer relevant to the campaign while the sensitive details remain undisclosed.

The distinction is more important than it may first appear. The campaign needs to know whether the criterion is met; it does not necessarily need a complete personal profile. A proof can separate those two ideas. The user establishes eligibility without turning the underlying data into a resource the advertiser can retain and use for other purposes. This supports precise ad targeting while maintaining user privacy.

That model changes the discussion from data acquisition to assertion verification. The advertiser defines a condition for the campaign. The user decides whether to prove that condition. The system is designed so that the condition can be confirmed without exposing the actual age or location in the example. This is a different privacy posture from buying detailed information through brokers and then relying on a platform to build the target audience.

ZKPs are not a reason to ignore the design of consent. The user still needs to understand the condition they are being asked to prove and the advertising exchange attached to it. But the technology provides a framework for reducing disclosure where a broad data profile is not necessary. That is a concrete way Web3 can try to reconcile advertisers' interest in relevance with users' interest in privacy.

## Direct creator monetization

Web3 also changes the position of creators. Creators can earn revenue directly from their audiences, reducing their reliance on traditional advertising. This matters because the conventional advertising model places creators within a chain where platform decisions, engagement incentives, and unclear payment flows can shape what work is rewarded. A more direct relationship gives creators another route for turning audience support into income.

Through [NFTs](/what-are-nfts) and token-gated content, creators can establish sustainable income streams from dedicated fans. These mechanisms make the relationship explicit. A fan can support a creator through an asset or by gaining access to content, rather than only by contributing attention that is later sold through an intermediary. The claim is not that advertising disappears. It is that creators have a way to reduce dependence on it.

The potential benefit is a healthier relationship between creators and communities. When income can come from dedicated fans, the incentive can move away from clickbait and toward producing high-quality content. That does not make quality automatic, and it does not mean every audience will choose a token-gated model. It gives creators an option to organize support around the people who value their work most directly.

For advertising, this has an important consequence. A creator who has direct revenue does not need to treat every decision solely as a way to maximize engagement for a platform. The creator can consider what serves the community that is providing support. This better aligns the interests of creator and audience, while offering advertisers a setting in which attention is not the only form of value in the relationship.

## Friction, incentives, and adoption

Transitioning to a Web3 advertising model presents significant challenges. Established Web2 companies will resist changes that threaten their dominance. This is an incentive problem as much as a technical one. Companies that benefit from controlling data, the advertising supply chain, and the relationship between users and advertisers have little reason to welcome a system that gives those parties more direct control.

User experience is an equally important constraint. Managing data permissions and engaging with crypto systems must improve for widespread adoption. A user-centered model cannot rely on users tolerating confusing choices simply because its principles are attractive. If permissions are difficult to understand, if the wallet interaction is unclear, or if the rewards are hard to use, the proposed gain in control may not feel meaningful in practice.

The direction toward enhanced privacy is nevertheless evident. Regulations such as GDPR and Apple's App Tracking Transparency are already challenging the traditional surveillance model. Web3 supplies a technological framework for an advertising model built on user consent, respect for privacy, and a more equitable distribution of value across the internet. It should be considered a framework, not an automatic replacement for the present system.

For marketers, the substantive change is in how attention is approached. Rather than relying on tracking as the default route to relevance, they can consider a model that rewards users for attention and requests data through clearer permission. For users, the proposed benefit is greater control over what is shared and what is received in return. For creators, direct monetization through NFTs and token-gated content offers another source of support. The future of digital advertising will depend on whether these elements can be made simple enough to use and strong enough to shift the incentives that define the current model.
