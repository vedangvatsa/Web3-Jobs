Thailand's Securities and Exchange Commission board has approved principles that would cap stablecoin transfers at 5 million baht per day and bar licensed digital asset operators from moving tokens to or from third-party wallets. The board decision, published as [SEC announcement No. 183/2026 dated Sept. 3](https://www.sec.or.th/EN/Pages/News_Detail.aspx?SECID=13294), sets out supervisory principles for stablecoin transactions conducted through licensed operators. A public hearing runs through September 2026, and reporting on the consultation [puts the comment deadline at Sept. 25](https://cryptobriefing.com/thailand-sec-stablecoin-transfer-cap/).

The core of the proposal is a same-customer rule. An account or wallet sending stablecoins into a customer's account at a licensed operator must be verified as belonging to that customer, and the destination of any withdrawal must be verified the same way. Moving tokens into an account from another person's wallet, or out of an account to another person's wallet, would be prohibited. Those wallets must also satisfy Travel Rule requirements, customer profiling, and screening designed to keep out mule accounts and parties linked to illegal activity, alongside blockchain analytics and monitoring tools that trace asset movements and flag connections to high-risk or watchlist wallets. Transfer values must additionally fit the customer's declared source of income and financial position.

Proving a wallet belongs to the customer will take more than a declaration. Operators will need documented procedures showing how control of each self-hosted wallet was established, with evidence standards, exception handling, and audit trails. They will also need to separate personal wallets from custodial accounts, business-controlled addresses, smart contracts, and addresses under shared control, since the requirement targets verified account holders collecting or forwarding assets for undisclosed third parties. The SEC page carrying the announcement also links a parallel public-comments document on the same principles and an earlier issuance on the digital-asset Travel Rule.

The 5 million baht ceiling, worth roughly $151,000, would apply separately to inbound and outbound transfers, per person, per operator, per day. The SEC release states that transfers between customer accounts at two supervised Thai operators would fall outside the cap, provided both the originating and destination operators apply Travel Rule requirements. The principles also cover market makers on digital asset exchanges, liquidity providers used by brokers, and off-platform transactions, bringing those channels under explicit supervisory standards.

"The SEC is committed to supervising the capital market and the digital asset market in a manner that keeps pace with relevant developments and risks," said Mrs. Pornanong Budsaratragoon, the SEC's secretary-general, in the announcement. She said the changes aim to stop inappropriate uses of stablecoins while protecting investors and supporting a credible, transparent Thai digital asset market, with hearing results feeding into further rule revisions.

The SEC said it has watched stablecoin volumes through licensed operators climb, particularly in USDT, alongside transaction patterns suggesting money laundering, cybercrime, and evasion of international money transfer rules. The regulator developed the principles with the Bank of Thailand, the Thai Digital Asset Operators Trade Association, and licensed operators. [Crypto Briefing reported](https://cryptobriefing.com/thailand-sec-stablecoin-transfer-cap/) that the Bank of Thailand flagged abnormal USDT trading volumes in July 2026, concerned that stablecoins were being used to sidestep banking disclosure requirements.

The consultation reaches beyond customer deposits and withdrawals. Exchanges would face market-maker supervision comparable to the securities business, with clearer disclosure of which firms supply liquidity in which assets. Liquidity providers and the source exchanges behind brokers would need oversight by a competent authority covering financial crime controls or business conduct. Off-platform broker services would face a 3 million baht minimum transaction value with published buy and sell prices, and brokers could not arrange off-platform trades directly between two customers, instead routing them to a licensed exchange for matching, according to [analysis of the proposals by FinCrime Central](https://fincrimecentral.com/thailand-sec-5m-baht-stablecoin-transfer-limit/). Exemptions would cover operators moving assets in their own name for business purposes, entities supervised by the Bank of Thailand under specific authorization, and qualifying market makers supporting token-baht pairs.

The ban on third-party movements would close off several current uses of regulated platforms. Peer-to-peer payments routed through exchange accounts and treasury setups that shift funds between different entities' wallets would no longer pass through licensed operators outside the exemptions for supervised counterparties. Every wallet touching a licensed operator's systems would need verification as the account holder's own. The income-consistency test adds a further layer: customers would need to show their transfers match their financial position, not only their identity.

The proposals also reach the firms behind the platforms. Exchanges would have to name their market makers and identify which assets each one supports, then screen, monitor, and periodically review them, including where their assets come from and what their trading is for. Liquidity providers and the source exchanges behind brokers would need supervision by a competent authority covering financial crime controls or business conduct. Providers could not sit in jurisdictions lacking measures that implement Financial Action Task Force recommendations. Brokers would have to disclose each provider's identity and any conflicts of interest to customers, and establish the source of assets and the true purpose behind transactions. Operators applying an exemption would need to confirm legal identity, regulatory status, business purpose, and the relevant authorization rather than taking a customer's label at face value. The SEC release numbers the decision as No. 183/2026 and dates it Sept. 3, with the board meeting held in September 2026.

The SEC would also gain a direct corrective tool. Where an operator fails to collect or disclose required information, the regulator could order corrections within a set period, followed by directions to take or avoid specific actions until complete and accurate information is available. Supervisors expect monitoring that looks past single transactions: cumulative flows over time, relationships between customers, shared wallet exposure, funds moving out rapidly after receipt, repeated transfers just below internal thresholds, and unexplained links to foreign platforms.

The perimeter has limits the SEC itself acknowledges through the design. Purely peer-to-peer transfers that never touch a licensed Thai operator sit outside the proposal. Customers could try to split activity across several operators, connected persons, or multiple self-hosted addresses. The SEC's answer, as described in the announcement, is aggregation and behavioral monitoring: operators would track cumulative daily flows, screen for mule accounts, trace blockchain exposure, and check whether transaction purpose and counterparties fit the customer's profile. Thailand's digital-asset Travel Rule, developed with the Anti-Money Laundering Office, takes effect on Feb. 27, 2027 with five-year record retention, giving supervisors originator and beneficiary data to work with. The principles remain a proposal until the hearing concludes, with public comments open until Sept. 25.
