Four Democratic senators asked Securities and Exchange Commission Chairman Paul Atkins to explain how anti-money-laundering rules apply to tokenized stock trading, warning that shares bought by verified investors can end up in unverified or offshore wallets. The senators want answers within 30 days, according to the [Nov. 16 press release](https://www.alsobrooks.senate.gov/news/press-releases/alsobrooks-leads-colleagues-in-pushing-sec-for-answers-transparency-on-tokenized-stock-change/) from Senator Angela Alsobrooks' office.

Alsobrooks of Maryland led Senators Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada, Mark Warner of Virginia and Ruben Gallego of Arizona in writing to Atkins about the SEC's Sept. 17, 2026 order, which exempts tokenized securities venues from most securities laws so they can trade tokenized US stocks. The release cited POLITICO's Morning Money for the campaign. The senators said the order, called the Innovation Exemption, took effect without public input first.

"We share the goal of ensuring U.S. capital markets and investors benefit from new technology," the four wrote, "and consider the Exemption with the most important shared principle: a tokenized security is a security." They said the exemption lacks clarity in three areas: illicit finance, market integrity and investor protection.

The sharpest questions concern money laundering. The exemption requires a venue to be a US person subject to OFAC sanctions, but sets no identity-verification standard and does not require an AML program, the letter said. Venues must disclose "applicable AML/CFT requirements" without saying which requirements apply or whether compliance is mandatory. The order does not make tokenized securities venues financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act.

Transfer rules are the second gap. The exemption requires venues to control who can trade but does not demand transfer restrictions inside the token contracts themselves. As a result, the senators wrote, a tokenized equity bought by a verified individual could be sent to an unverified wallet, "including one held by a foreign person or illicit actor, beyond the reach of the U.S. regulatory perimeter." The letter asks which entity would be responsible once a token leaves the venue and whether the SEC has the authority to trace, freeze or recover it.

The lawmakers also counted the objections. More than sixty public companies have objected to having their shares tokenized, the letter said. Issuers have 30 days after a venue notifies them of an intent to tokenize and list their stock to object, with no way to file a standing objection, object after the window closes or withdraw consent later. [Cointelegraph reported](https://cointelegraph.com/news/us-senators-sec-tokenized-stock-safeguards) that a Nov. 5 public notice from OKXICE, the joint venture between OKX and Intercontinental Exchange, listed 67 companies that objected to their shares trading on the venue.

On retail investor protections, the letter notes that platforms trading under the exemption can operate without complying with Regulation NMS, so they face no requirement to display best prices or report trades to the market in real time. It also asks what legal rights a holder of a tokenized equity has, what happens if the tokenizer becomes insolvent, and how the SEC is ensuring tokenized securities are backed by the underlying shares.

Small issuers get attention too. The letter asks whether the SEC will weigh the compliance cost on small public companies that must respond to repeated tokenization requests from different venues, and whether the agency will keep a central public repository tracking those requests so issuers and the public can find every venue planning to trade a stock token. The senators also ask whether an issuer could file one standing objection covering all venues, or require its affirmative consent before third-party tokens of its stock may trade.

The senators asked 13 questions in total, covering enforcement of AML rules, custody of the underlying assets, and whether the SEC will commit to notice-and-comment rulemaking before making the exemption permanent. Retail investors who buy directly from a venue without a broker-dealer may also lose protections the letter lists by name, including Securities Investor Protection Corporation insurance coverage and the advertising rules that apply to registered brokers, since the exemption says tokenized holdings are "deemed to provide the same rights and privileges" as the underlying shares without spelling out which protections follow. Cointelegraph noted that Atkins said in September that federal anti-fraud and anti-manipulation rules would continue to apply in full under the exemption.

The exemption lets venues trade tokenized stocks without registering as securities exchanges, and the senators asked the SEC to respond to each question within thirty days and to pursue any future changes through a transparent rulemaking process.
