Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller told a payments conference in New York that traditional settlement rails and central bank money will keep a central role as financial assets move onto blockchains. Speaking at The Clearing House's annual conference on Nov. 17, Waller said that central bank money "need not be used in every transaction or on every platform, but its presence at the foundation of the system is essential," according to the text of the [speech posted by the Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261117a.htm).

Waller argued that the private sector is building a multi-instrument ecosystem of tokenized money that includes stablecoins, tokenized deposits and tokenized money market funds, each with different tradeoffs in issuer model, regulatory treatment and reach. He pointed to progress already visible: stablecoins are being used behind the scenes to settle obligations among participants in card networks, and The Clearing House is one of several groups building a network for clearing and settling tokenized deposits. A convergence between blockchain technologies and established markets is clearly under way, he said, with stocks, bonds and commodities now being tokenized for use on blockchain networks.

The speech described how tokenized money could change market mechanics. An investor could buy a tokenized stock on a Saturday, when traditional systems are closed, and program the payment and the asset to change hands simultaneously, reducing settlement delays. Waller also cautioned that atomic settlement, which completes all steps of a trade at once or not at all, is not the only model: net settlement and batch settlement will stay useful where saving liquidity matters, such as in high-volume capital market activity.

Waller built the case from first principles, defining tokenization as altering the properties of one form of asset so it can be transferred on a different platform, and a smart contract as code embedded in a database that executes only when all its conditions are met at the same time. He said his remarks were informed by ongoing discussions with industry participants, including a payments innovation roundtable he hosted at the Federal Reserve with people from traditional finance and decentralized finance. The governor also cast the Fed's role as complementary to the private sector: the central bank can provide core payment and settlement infrastructure on which private firms innovate, assess risks to financial stability, and keep engaging with the industry to understand market challenges.

Waller framed traditional rails as the backbone that makes those new systems trustworthy. Large-value payment systems and newer instant payment systems have proven resilient through market events, he said, and they provide access to the safest forms of dollars through links to central bank money. That makes them a reliable base for final settlement on which tokenized services can develop, including today's on- and off-ramping of stablecoins. He encouraged further conversation on ways tokenized markets and traditional rails can connect and complement one another.

The governor placed the moment in a longer arc, writing that the market is in an early period of experimentation and convergence between new and existing systems, and that participants may one day look back on it the way they view the payment, clearing and settlement technologies of the 19th and 20th centuries. He also flagged coming work on how tokenization and blockchain-based systems will interact with artificial intelligence agents in payments and commerce.

Alongside the speech, Waller called for a more aggressive move on interest rates. In a CNBC interview on Wednesday, he said the Fed should deliver a 50-basis-point increase at its Dec. 8-9 meeting or explicitly signal another hike in January, [Decrypt reported](https://decrypt.co/383566/fed-waller-50-basis-point-rate-hike-tokenized-finance-banks). "My view is then you have to go 50 in December to put it in place and get it done," Waller said, warning that insufficiently aggressive action could weaken expectations that inflation returns to the Fed's target. He added that policymakers might need to "shock people" to stop businesses and consumers from accepting inflation near 3% as normal.

Three straight monthly core CPI readings of 0.3% have intensified his concern about persistent inflation, a pace of roughly 3.5% annually against a 2% target measured by the separate personal consumption expenditures index. Waller set that threshold in a Nov. 10 speech, saying another 0.3% core reading would warrant considering a half-point hike, and October's data met it with core prices up 0.3% and headline prices up 0.4%. The increase he is urging would lift the benchmark rate from its current 3.75%-4% range to 4.25%-4.50%. Futures pricing Wednesday morning showed roughly a 95% probability of a smaller 25-basis-point move and about a 5% probability of the half-point option.
