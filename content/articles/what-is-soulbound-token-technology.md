The field of **[NFTs](/what-are-nfts)** has primarily focused on transferability. An NFT's value is often connected to its ability to be bought and sold in an open market. That property is useful when an asset is meant to change hands. It becomes harder to justify when the asset is supposed to stand for a person's own achievement, affiliation, or history.

A paper co-authored by [Ethereum](/what-is-ethereum) founder Vitalik Buterin introduced a different concept: **Soulbound [Tokens](/what-is-a-token) (SBTs)**. An SBT is described as a type of [NFT](/what-are-nfts) that is **non-transferable**. Once assigned to a particular [wallet](/how-to-choose-a-crypto-wallet), called a "Soul," it remains permanently bound to that wallet. The design changes the question from whether an item can be traded to whether a record can remain associated with the wallet that received it.

That distinction is the basis for the proposed use of SBTs in digital identity, reputation, and affiliations on the [blockchain](/what-is-a-blockchain). The concept is not a claim that a wallet is the same thing as a person. It is a proposal for attaching certain records to a wallet in a way that does not permit their routine transfer. Understanding the proposal requires keeping both parts of that description in view: the record is on-chain, and the relationship between the record and the wallet is meant to persist.

## Why transferability is a poor fit for identity

Transferability is not a defect in every NFT. It is central to the purpose of an asset intended for exchange. A problem emerges when a transferable token is used to symbolize a personal achievement. Consider a university degree or a professional certification. If the token standing for that credential could be sold, the purchaser could acquire the visible marker without having earned the underlying achievement. The credential's integrity would be undermined.

The problem is not merely technical. A degree, a professional certification, an employment record, or a record of participation is meaningful because of the relationship between the person and the event or institution. If that relationship can be transferred as readily as a collectible, the token no longer reliably represents the thing it was meant to indicate. A market price may be coherent for a transferable asset, but it is a poor test of whether a person completed a course of study, held a role, or participated in a community.

This is why standard, transferable NFTs cannot adequately represent personal accomplishments or aspects of identity. A token can be visually unique and still fail as a credential if its ownership can be reassigned. The SBT proposal addresses that mismatch by making non-transferability the defining property. The token is not meant to be a portable item of value; it is meant to be a durable record associated with its recipient's Soul.

The practical implication is narrow but significant. An SBT can make a claim about a wallet's history easier to inspect, provided the issuer and the claim are meaningful to the person reviewing it. It cannot eliminate the need to ask what the token records, who issued it, or why the record matters. Non-transferability addresses one credibility problem. It does not turn every token into a complete account of a person's identity.

## What a Soul is meant to collect

In this model, a digital Soul is essentially an Ethereum wallet that accumulates SBTs from different sources over time. The word "accumulates" matters. A single token may say little by itself. The broader idea is that a collection of non-transferable records could form a more detailed account of achievements, affiliations, and reputation than one isolated credential.

The examples illustrate the range of records the proposal has in view:

- **Education:** A university issues an SBT representing a degree.
- **Employment:** An employer provides an SBT confirming employment.
- **Community:** A [DAO](/what-is-a-dao) issues an SBT acknowledging governance participation.
- **Credentials:** A conference provides a POAP, or Proof of Attendance Protocol, a specific type of SBT verifying attendance.

Each example has an issuer, a recipient Soul, and a stated basis for the record. A university is positioned to issue a record about a degree because the token is meant to reflect the university's own action. An employer is positioned to confirm employment. A DAO can acknowledge governance participation. A conference can verify attendance through a POAP. The model is therefore not simply a way for an individual to declare a credential. It depends on institutions and communities issuing records about activity they can identify.

That dependence creates an important analytical limit. The existence of a token does not answer every question about the underlying event. A reader still needs to know what the issuer intended the token to confirm and whether that issuer is relevant to the context. A token representing attendance is not the same claim as a token representing a degree. A record of governance participation is not the same claim as a record of employment. The value of the collection comes from reading the records in context, not from treating every SBT as interchangeable proof.

The Soul is also a wallet, which makes key management central rather than incidental. The proposed identity is attached to the wallet's control mechanism. That is useful for creating a stable association, but it means the history collected in the Soul is tied to the user's ability to retain access to that wallet. The idea of a self-owned digital identity is thus connected to a demanding practical responsibility: preserving access to the Soul and its records.

## The proposed on-chain resume

The aggregation of SBTs within a Soul could create a rich, self-owned digital identity. The shorthand is an on-chain [resume](/how-to-build-a-web3-resume-that-stands-out), but the analogy should be used carefully. A conventional resume is usually a curated account written for a particular opportunity. A collection of SBTs would instead be a record accumulated from issuers over time. The two may overlap, yet they do not perform exactly the same function.

The potential advantage of the on-chain record is that an individual could demonstrate reputation and expertise without depending on a centralized platform such as LinkedIn. The record would be associated with the Soul rather than held solely within one platform's profile. That framing emphasizes ownership and portability of the identity record, even while each specific SBT is non-transferable.

This distinction also helps explain why a collection could be more useful than a single credential. An employment SBT might indicate one relationship. A governance-participation SBT might indicate another. A degree token and an attendance token would describe different events. Taken together, they could show a pattern of affiliations and activity. Taken carelessly, they could create the illusion of completeness when they only show what particular issuers chose to record. A responsible use of the concept should allow the record to be interpreted rather than treating it as a final score.

The phrase "self-owned" should not obscure the role of the issuers. The user holds the Soul, but the meaning of an SBT is connected to the entity that issued it. If a university issues a degree record, the relevant fact is not only that the Soul contains a token. It is that the university made a particular attestation. In the same way, a DAO's acknowledgement of participation has meaning because the DAO is the source of the acknowledgement. SBTs offer a format for these records; they do not remove the need for judgment about their source.

## Reputation and governance applications

One proposed application is trust within a community. A DAO might grant voting power based on the collection of reputation-based SBTs in a Soul rather than merely on the number of tokens held. The logic is straightforward: if a DAO wants voting power to reflect participation or reputation, it can look for records of those qualities instead of relying only on asset ownership.

This approach is notable because it separates financial holdings from every possible measure of influence. It does not say that token holdings are irrelevant, nor does it guarantee that a reputation record is fair. It offers a different input for governance decisions. The design question becomes which forms of participation a DAO wants to recognize, who can issue the relevant SBTs, and how a collection of records should affect voting power.

The benefit is conditional on clear definitions. If the purpose is to acknowledge governance participation, the token needs to represent that specific fact rather than a vague notion of loyalty. If the purpose is to weight votes, members need to understand how the weight is determined. Without those boundaries, a reputation-based system can become harder to interpret than the token-based measure it was meant to supplement.

SBTs also put a useful question to any identity system: what should be visible, and to whom? A record that is valuable for one governance decision may not be appropriate to disclose in another setting. The promise of an on-chain identity record is not that every affiliation must become relevant everywhere. The proposal raises the possibility of richer signals, which makes the choice of signal more consequential.

## Airdrops, sybil resistance, and lending

Another proposed application concerns token distribution. A project could [airdrop](/understanding-airdrop-campaigns-in-web3) tokens only to Souls that possess SBTs from trusted communities. The stated aim is to make it more difficult for sybil attackers, who use multiple wallets to gain an unfair advantage. A non-transferable record from a trusted community gives the project a criterion that is different from the simple existence of a wallet.

This is not the same as a guarantee that abuse disappears. It is a way to raise the relevance of a distribution criterion by tying it to a record that cannot be routinely sold or moved to another wallet. The project still has to decide which communities it trusts and what the SBT signifies. A token sent to a Soul is only as meaningful as the selection logic behind it. The SBT concept makes the selection rule more specific; it does not make the rule self-executing as a matter of judgment.

Undercollateralized lending is a further, explicitly future-facing possibility. A [DeFi](/what-is-defi) protocol might offer loans to Souls with a strong on-chain history of reliable borrowing and repayment, evidenced by SBTs from other lending protocols. In that model, the records would establish a form of on-chain credit score. The proposal is significant because it treats repayment history as a possible signal where traditional collateral is absent or reduced.

The qualification in that proposal is important. The article describes what future protocols might do; it does not present undercollateralized lending based on SBTs as an established outcome. Any use of such a record would need to decide what counts as reliable history and how a lender interprets it. A token-based credit signal could simplify one part of that assessment while introducing questions about privacy, error, and the consequences of a persistent record. The SBT idea makes those questions unavoidable rather than resolving them by itself.

## How non-transferability might be implemented

Technically, SBTs would likely extend existing NFT standards such as ERC-721 while restricting the `transfer` and `approve` functions. The essential characteristic is that the token remains permanently tied to the wallet that receives it. This description is concise, but its purpose is clear: if the holder cannot transfer or approve a transfer, the token cannot operate like a conventional tradable NFT.

The technical restriction is a means to support the social claim made by the token. A degree record should not be transferable because the degree is not supposed to be transferable. An employment record, governance acknowledgement, or attendance credential has the same logic when it is intended to reflect the recipient's own relationship with the issuer. The technology encodes a boundary that the credential requires.

At the same time, a technical restriction cannot explain every human consequence of the record. The contract can restrict a function; it cannot decide whether the token was issued appropriately, whether the underlying claim is accurate, or whether a user should have to display it. Those questions remain part of the design and governance of any SBT system. The implementation matters, but the surrounding rules and expectations give the implementation its purpose.

## The unresolved issues are central

Soulbound Tokens remain in an early theoretical phase, and the open questions are not peripheral details. They determine whether a permanent wallet-bound record can be useful without becoming harmful or brittle.

**Key management** is the first issue. If a user loses the private key to their Soul, they may lose access to their identity record and every bound token it holds. A reliable recovery solution is needed. The article identifies a possible social-recovery mechanism, but the need is broader than a label: a recovery path must preserve the value of a bound record while addressing the loss of wallet access. Without a credible answer, permanence can turn from a benefit into a failure mode.

**Privacy** is the second issue. A public record of affiliations can have negative privacy implications. The more records a Soul accumulates, the more detailed the visible account may become. That detail is central to the identity proposal, yet it also creates the risk that information intended for one context will be read in another. A system that values a rich record must take seriously the possibility that people need limits on what that record reveals.

**Negative SBTs** raise a third question. If an unwanted "bad" SBT representing a negative affiliation is sent to a Soul, the recipient needs a way to conceal or reject it. The concern shows why receipt alone cannot be treated as consent. A non-transferable record is particularly difficult when the holder did not want it and cannot simply move it elsewhere. Any useful model must account for the recipient's ability to avoid an imposed identity claim.

These issues point to the same conclusion: the appeal of SBTs lies in creating durable associations, but durability changes the stakes of error, loss, and unwanted disclosure. The proposal cannot be evaluated only by asking whether a token can be transferred. It also requires attention to who controls the Soul, who can issue a record, what the record reveals, and how a person responds when the record is wrong or unwanted.

Soulbound Tokens remain an intriguing way to think about digital identity. By moving beyond purely financial and transferable assets, they propose a more specific and human-centered [Web3](/what-is-web3) and support the idea of a "Decentralized Society." Their significance is not that they make identity simple. It is that they put identity, reputation, affiliation, recovery, and privacy into the design of the token itself.
